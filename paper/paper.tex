\author{Mahdi Saeedi}
\affiliation{%
  \institution{USC-ISI}
  \country{}

\begin{abstract}
We evaluate prompt-induced social conformity in six OLMo-3 7B checkpoints (Base, Instruct, Instruct-SFT, Instruct-DPO, Think, Think-SFT) across six temperatures ($T\in\{0.0,0.2,\dots,1.0\}$) and three behavioral conditions (Control, Asch-style peer consensus, and explicit authority). Our analysis includes 41{,}505 judge-labeled trials. We find that social pressure reliably raises pooled factual error for every tested variant, but with strongly variant-dependent profiles: Instruct-SFT shows the largest peer effect ($\Delta$Asch $=+22.6$ pp), Instruct/Instruct-DPO show authority-dominant asymmetry (Instruct: +7.6 vs.\ +2.3 pp; Instruct-DPO: +5.7 vs.\ +2.9 pp), and Think/Think-SFT have the lowest control error (37.0--38.1\%) yet large pressure penalties (+17.6 to +20.8 pp). Conditional metrics reinforce this picture: Instruct-SFT has the highest Asch truth-override rate (0.691), while Instruct-DPO has the lowest Asch wrong-answer flip rate (0.294). Effects are heterogeneous by topic and temperature, including pockets of negative pressure deltas in science/truthfulness. On the social-conventions subset, wrong-answer agreement rises sharply under pressure (e.g., Base 2.5\%$\rightarrow$57.9--61.5\%; Instruct-SFT 5.6\%$\rightarrow$58.3--76.4\%).
\end{abstract}

\keywords{social conformity, sycophancy, large language models, RLHF, temperature, alignment}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into decision-critical workflows---from clinical diagnostics to legal adjudication---demands a rigorous interrogation of their reliability under social pressure. A persistent structural failure mode is ``social conformity'' or ``sycophancy,'' defined as the propensity of an AI system to prioritize agreement with the user's worldview over its own internal knowledge or objective reality~\cite{sharma2024sycophancy}. Unlike hallucinations, which are stochastic errors of fact, sycophancy is a structural alignment failure where the model may \textit{know} the truth but suppresses it to satisfy the perceived intent of the user.

For this reason, our primary conformity signal is \textbf{truth override}: $P(Y^{(p)}=0 \mid Y^{(c)}=1)$. This conditional metric isolates exactly the cases where the model is correct in Control, then becomes incorrect under social pressure on the same item. In other words, it measures knowledge suppression under pressure, not generic baseline error. We therefore treat pooled error deltas as descriptive context and truth override as the core behavioral measure of sycophancy.

This behavior mirrors the phenomenon of social conformity observed in human psychology, most famously by Solomon Asch in the 1950s. Asch demonstrated that individuals would frequently endorse patently false statements (e.g., misidentifying the length of a line) if surrounded by a group of peers doing the same~\cite{asch1956}. A recent replication by Franzen and Mader (2023) confirmed a human error rate of approximately 33\% under group pressure, which drops to 25\% when monetary incentives favor accuracy~\cite{franzen2023asch}. In the context of Human-AI Interaction (HAI), the ``peer group'' is simulated by the user's prompt history and context window. If a user confidently asserts a misconception, the LLM, trained to be ``helpful'' and ``harmless,'' often defaults to validation rather than correction.

The prevalence of this behavior appears intrinsic to the dominant alignment paradigm: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). As detailed by Sharma et al. (2024), human annotators---the ultimate arbiters of reward signals in RLHF---consistently rate ``convincingly written sycophantic responses'' higher than truthful but corrective ones~\cite{sharma2024sycophancy}. Consequently, models learn to maximize user satisfaction, potentially at the expense of accuracy.

This paper uses the OLMo-3 ecosystem from the Allen Institute for AI (Ai2) as a ``glass box'' setting to compare checkpoints spanning pre-training, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), preference optimization (DPO), and reasoning-focused training~\cite{olmo3}. Our evidence is observational rather than causal: we compare behavioral profiles across released checkpoints under matched prompt pressure. The central empirical pattern is asymmetric conformity across checkpoints: Instruct-SFT is associated with stronger peer-consensus pressure effects, while Instruct and Instruct-DPO are associated with stronger authority-prompt effects. We address three research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Training Method Effect:} Does the training methodology (base, instruction-tuning, SFT, DPO, chain-of-thought) systematically affect epistemic independence under social pressure?
    \item \textbf{The Temperature Hypothesis:} Does sampling temperature ($T$) influence the model's susceptibility to social pressure, and does this interaction vary by model variant?
    \item \textbf{The Social Pressure Pattern:} Is social conformity higher under implicit peer consensus (Asch-style) or explicit authoritative claims?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{The Psychology of Artificial Conformity}

The theoretical foundation of this study bridges computational linguistics and social psychology. The classic Asch Conformity Experiments revealed that social pressure could override sensory evidence~\cite{asch1956}. Cheng et al. (2025) propose the ELEPHANT benchmark and broaden LLM sycophancy beyond factual correctness to social face-preservation: across advice and moral scenarios, they find that LLMs preserve user face by 45 percentage points more than humans and still choose to preserve face in 48\% of moral-conflict cases~\cite{elephant2025}. They interpret this behavior through \textit{Face Theory}---the sociolinguistic imperative to preserve the user's positive social standing---suggesting that sycophancy may be especially prevalent in socially normative domains.
\par
Recent work expands measurement beyond single-turn factual flips. Hong et al. introduce \textsc{SYCON Bench}, a multi-turn evaluation suite that quantifies how quickly models conform to sustained user pressure (Turn of Flip) and how often they reverse their stance (Number of Flip); across 17 LLMs, they find that alignment tuning amplifies sycophancy while scaling and reasoning optimization improve resistance, and that adopting a third-person perspective can reduce sycophancy by up to 63.8\% in a debate scenario~\cite{hong2025syconbench}. Beyond explicit social pressure, sycophancy can be induced by rhetorical framing or partial evidence: Rrv et al. show that misleading keywords can elicit factually incorrect but agreeable completions, and evaluate defense strategies adapted from hallucination mitigation~\cite{rrv2024chaos}; Kaur demonstrates argument-driven stance shifts in political prompts, with sycophancy intensity correlating with argument strength~\cite{kaur2025echoes}. Finally, multi-agent settings introduce additional conformity pressures; Pitre et al. identify inter-agent sycophancy in multi-agent debate and propose prompt refinement to mitigate it~\cite{pitre2025consensagent}.
\par
Conformity effects have also been observed in higher-stakes domains. Shoval et al. adapt Asch-style social influence to psychiatric assessments and show that increasing social pressure can degrade LLM decision quality even when a system prompt emphasizes correctness over agreement~\cite{shoval2025psychiatric}. Complementarily, Weng et al. study conformity in multi-agent LLM settings and introduce \textsc{BenchForm}, highlighting that social influence can emerge from group protocols (e.g., repeated discussion and majority signals) rather than a single user prompt~\cite{weng2025benchform}. Finally, beyond ``sycophancy'' framed as agreement with a user, Braun documents \textit{acquiescence bias} (a tendency to answer ``yes'' or agree) as a distinct conversational bias in LLMs, relevant to our opinion-suite agreement proxy under injected consensus~\cite{braun2025acquiescence}.

\subsection{The Mechanics of RLHF}

Prior work links RLHF to sycophancy tendencies. Sharma et al. (2024) analyzed preference datasets and found that humans often prefer responses that match their views~\cite{sharma2024sycophancy}. RLHF for LLM assistants is typically implemented as preference optimization on top of supervised instruction tuning (e.g., the InstructGPT pipeline~\cite{ouyang2022instructgpt}), using policy-gradient methods such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} or offline objectives such as DPO~\cite{rafailov2023dpo}. Kirk et al. show that RLHF can reduce generation diversity and sharpen the output distribution~\cite{rlhf_entropy}, which may increase the likelihood of sampling socially ``safe'' agreement modes at low temperatures.

\subsection{Sampling Temperature and Inference Dynamics}

Sampling temperature is a hyperparameter governing the randomness of token selection. A temperature of 0.0 (greedy decoding) selects the argmax token, while higher temperatures flatten the logits, increasing diversity. Renze (2024) argued that temperature changes (0.0 to 1.0) do not statistically impact problem-solving performance in neutral contexts~\cite{renze2024}. However, this finding may not hold in \textit{biased} contexts. If RLHF has shifted the mode of the distribution toward sycophancy, low-temperature sampling should maximize conformity. Conversely, higher temperatures might allow the model to sample from the ``tail'' of the distribution where alternative responses reside.

\section{Methodology}
\sloppy

\subsection{Model Selection: The Olmo-3 Ecosystem}

The primary subject of this analysis is the \textbf{OLMo-3} family of 7B-parameter models, released by the Allen Institute for AI in late 2025~\cite{olmo3}. In the evaluation dataset used here, six variants have complete cross-temperature behavioral coverage:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Base} (allenai/Olmo-3-1025-7B): Pre-trained foundation model without alignment.
    \item \textbf{Instruct} (allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Instruct): Instruction-tuned with RLHF.
    \item \textbf{Instruct-SFT} (allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Instruct-SFT): Additional supervised fine-tuning.
    \item \textbf{Instruct-DPO} (allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Instruct-DPO): Instruction-tuned with Direct Preference Optimization~\cite{rafailov2023dpo}.
    \item \textbf{Think} (allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Think): Chain-of-thought reasoning variant.
    \item \textbf{Think-SFT} (allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Think-SFT): Think variant with additional supervised fine-tuning.
\end{itemize}
We do not report Think-DPO or RL-Zero in the main analysis because they are not present with complete coverage in the analyzed dataset.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

We adapted the Asch conformity paradigm to evaluate LLM behavior under simulated social pressure. The design of our prompt-type variations---spanning peer-consensus framing, authority framing, tone (plain, neutral, confident, uncertain), participant diversity, and devil's-advocate configurations---is inspired by the prompt taxonomy introduced by Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2024conformity}.

\subsubsection{Conditions}
We use three experimental conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Control}: The model receives a factual question with no social context.
    \item \textbf{Asch (5 Confederates)}: Five simulated ``users'' unanimously provide the same incorrect answer before the model responds, creating implicit peer consensus.
    \item \textbf{Authoritative Bias}: A single user authoritatively claims the incorrect answer, testing susceptibility to explicit authority.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Temperature Settings}
Each condition is evaluated at six temperature values:
\begin{itemize}
	    \item $T=0.0$: Deterministic greedy decoding (most ``confident'')
	    \item $T \in \{0.2, 0.4\}$: Low stochastic sampling
	    \item $T \in \{0.6, 0.8\}$: Higher stochastic sampling
	    \item $T=1.0$: Full stochastic sampling (most exploratory)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Dataset}
We use an expanded evaluation suite with up to \textbf{50 items per dataset}. Factual items are drawn from six topic bins: general (curated immutable facts), math (GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} and MMLU-math~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}), science (MMLU-science~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}), knowledge (MMLU-knowledge~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}), truthfulness (TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa}), and reasoning (ARC~\cite{clark2018arc}). Each factual dataset contributes up to 50 items per condition-temperature cell; math comprises \textbf{two} datasets and thus up to 100 items per cell. The social-conventions subset (\textbf{opinion}) contributes up to 50 items per cell; we report its conformity signal primarily via wrong-answer agreement.

Each item includes an injected wrong answer distinct from the ground-truth answer. In the Asch and Authority conditions, the confederates/user assert this wrong answer with high confidence; in Control, no social context is provided.

\subsubsection{Sample Size}
\begin{itemize}
	    \item Items per (variant, condition, temperature): up to 350 factual + 50 social-conventions items.
	    \item \textbf{Nominal design}: 6 variants $\times$ 6 temperatures $\times$ 3 conditions $\times$ 400 items $= 43{,}200$ trials.
	    \item \textbf{Observed analyzed trials}: 41{,}505 judge-labeled trials (96.1\% of nominal).
	    \item \textbf{Cell size variability}: realized per-cell counts vary (364--397) after filtering invalid/missing labels.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics}

We define the following metrics. Let $Y^{(c)} \in \{0,1\}$ denote factual correctness under Control and $Y^{(p)}$ correctness under a pressure condition (Asch or Authority). Let $W \in \{0,1\}$ denote \textit{wrong-answer endorsement}: whether a post-hoc \textbf{LLM judge} labels the response as endorsing the injected wrong answer (refusals treated as $Y=0$ and $W=0$); we write $W^{(c)}$ for Control and $W^{(p)}$ for a pressure condition.

\textbf{Judge labels ($Y,W$).} For each trial, we use the structured parsed judge JSON, which contains the fields is\_correct, wrong\_answer\_endorsed, and refusal\_flag. The judge model is Gemma 3 1B. We drop trials where this JSON is missing or fails to parse (yielding the judge-label coverage reported above). We retain the runner's deterministic score only for audit and debugging, and do not use it in the reported results.

\small
\begin{align*}
\text{\bfseries Error Rate} &= \#\text{incorrect} \,/\, \#\text{trials}. \\
\text{\bfseries Pressure Effect (Asch)} &= \text{Error}_{\text{Asch}} - \text{Error}_{\text{Control}}. \\
\text{\bfseries Pressure Effect (Authority)} &= \text{Error}_{\text{Authority}} - \text{Error}_{\text{Control}}. \\
\text{\bfseries Truth Override} &= P\!\left(Y^{(p)}=0 \mid Y^{(c)}=1\right). \\
\text{\bfseries Truth Rescue} &= P\!\left(Y^{(p)}=1 \mid Y^{(c)}=0\right). \\
\text{\bfseries Wrong-Answer Flip} &= P\!\left(W^{(p)}=1 \mid W^{(c)}=0\right). \\
\text{\bfseries Wrong-Answer Agreement} &= P(W=1). \\
&\qquad \text{(opinion items; computed per condition).}
\end{align*}
\normalsize

A positive pressure effect indicates that social context \textit{increased} errors (conformity); a negative value indicates that social context \textit{decreased} errors (corrective behavior). Truth override isolates conformity by conditioning on control-correct items (a behavioral proxy for ``knew the truth in Control, then flipped''), which is critical when baseline error rates are high. Conversely, truth rescue conditions on control-incorrect items and quantifies whether social context ever \textit{helps} the model become correct.

Some truth override cells are \textit{undefined} when the denominator is zero (no control-correct items). We treat these as explicit NA and report denominators alongside conditional metrics to avoid over-interpreting low-powered cells. Finally, because exact-match correctness can be conservative on long-form answers (e.g., TruthfulQA), we also compute a ground-truth-free factual conformity metric: wrong-answer flip rate, which measures adoption of the injected wrong answer under pressure among items where Control did not already endorse the wrong answer. For the social-conventions subset, we foreground wrong-answer agreement in the main narrative for consistency with the behavioral analysis figures.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Because the same items are evaluated across conditions, our core conformity metrics are item-conditioned (truth override, truth rescue, wrong-answer flip). We primarily report pooled effect sizes from the behavioral analysis tables and avoid strong causal interpretation across training stages. Where we compare pressure types, we report directional asymmetries (peer vs.\ authority) and explicitly note heterogeneity across topic and temperature.

\subsection{Factual error under social pressure}

Table~\ref{tab:factual_pooled_error} shows a consistent pooled pattern: all six variants have higher factual error under both pressure conditions than under control. The magnitudes, however, differ sharply by variant. Think/Think-SFT have the lowest control error (37.0--38.1\%) but large pressure penalties (+17.6 to +20.8 pp). At this checkpoint stage, Instruct-SFT shows the largest peer-associated increase (+22.6 pp), while Instruct and Instruct-DPO show larger authority-associated than peer-associated increases (+7.6 vs.\ +2.3 pp and +5.7 vs.\ +2.9 pp, respectively).

Figure~\ref{fig:factual_error_rates} also shows important heterogeneity: many cells are strongly positive, but not all. Across variant$\times$temperature$\times$topic cells, 15.5\% of Asch deltas and 15.5\% of authority deltas are negative (pressure improves error rate in those cells), so pressure effects are robust in aggregate but not universal.

\subsection{Temperature effects are real but non-monotonic}

Temperature changes effect size, but not in a simple monotonic direction. Instruct-SFT's Asch delta stays high across all temperatures (+18.2 to +24.5 pp). Think and Think-SFT peak near mid temperatures (around $T=0.4$--$0.6$), then decline at $T=1.0$. Instruct shows a sign flip for Asch at $T=0.6$ (negative pooled Asch delta at that temperature), while authority remains positive at every temperature for all six variants in pooled-by-variant summaries. This pattern argues against ``just raise temperature'' as a universal mitigation strategy (see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:temp_curve_appendix}).

\subsection{Peer vs.\ authority patterns via conditional metrics}

Conditional metrics separate ``knew in control, then flipped'' from baseline error. Pooled across temperatures and factual categories, Instruct-SFT has the highest Asch truth-override rate (0.691) and a marked peer$>$authority asymmetry (0.691 vs.\ 0.520). Instruct and Instruct-DPO show the opposite pattern: authority$>$peer in both truth override (Instruct 0.530 vs.\ 0.477; Instruct-DPO 0.528 vs.\ 0.441) and wrong-answer flip (Instruct 0.418 vs.\ 0.317; Instruct-DPO 0.412 vs.\ 0.294). Think and Think-SFT are more symmetric across pressure type (flip rates near 0.48 in both conditions). \subsection{Topic sensitivity}

Pressure effects are heterogeneous across factual categories. Pooled across variants and temperatures, reasoning shows the largest pressure-induced error increase (Asch: +17.8 pp; Authority: +20.3 pp), while science shows the smallest (+4.9 and +4.1 pp). General and knowledge remain high (+14.0/+11.8 pp and +15.1/+11.8 pp). The full topic-by-variant heatmap (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:topic_heatmap_appendix}) also reveals sign reversals in some science/truthfulness cells, especially for Instruct and Instruct-DPO.

\subsection{Opinion-suite wrong-answer agreement}

On the social-conventions subset, wrong-answer agreement rises sharply under pressure. Pooled across temperatures, Base rises from 2.5\% (Control) to 57.9\% (Asch) and 61.5\% (Authority); Instruct-SFT rises from 5.6\% to 76.4\% (Asch) and 58.3\% (Authority). Instruct and Instruct-DPO again show authority-dominant asymmetry on agreement (Instruct: 5.8\%$\rightarrow$30.8\% vs.\ 48.8\%; Instruct-DPO: 6.2\%$\rightarrow$26.2\% vs.\ 49.0\%). Think-family variants show high agreement under both pressures (e.g., Think-SFT: 16.3\%$\rightarrow$60.9\% and 68.8\%), alongside elevated refusal rates, indicating that abstention and conformity can co-occur.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The strongest evidence-backed conclusion is not simply that ``pressure hurts''; it is that \emph{pressure response profiles differ by checkpoint stage}. In this released-checkpoint comparison, Instruct-SFT is associated with larger peer-consensus deltas, Instruct/Instruct-DPO are associated with relatively larger authority-framing deltas, and Think-family models combine low baseline error with large pressure-associated degradation. Figure~\ref{fig:training_traj_main} shows how truth-override rates vary across the training trajectory under each pressure type.

Three caveats are important for interpretation. First, this analysis covers six variants with complete coverage and should not be read as a claim about missing checkpoints. Second, all outcomes depend on an LLM judge, so residual label noise remains possible despite structured JSON filtering. Third, temperature and topic effects are heterogeneous, including negative deltas in several cells, so any single pooled number masks substantial context dependence.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{fig_training_trajectory.png}
    \caption{Training-stage trajectory of pooled truth-override rates under Asch and Authority pressure.}
    \label{fig:training_traj_main}
\end{figure*}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In our OLMo-3 evaluation, prompt-induced conformity is a robust aggregate effect with checkpoint-specific profiles: Instruct-SFT is associated with stronger peer-consensus effects, Instruct/Instruct-DPO with stronger authority-associated effects, and Think-family models combine low baseline error with large pressure penalties. Temperature changes these effects but does not provide a universal mitigation, and topic-level heterogeneity is substantial. For LLM evaluation and alignment, this implies that conformity should be measured as a profile over pressure type, domain, and decoding regime, not as a single scalar score.

\subsection{Judge Labeling Protocol}

All reported behavioral metrics use a post-hoc LLM judge (Gemma 3 1B) and require valid parsed judge JSON. The judge receives the question, ground truth, injected wrong answer, and model completion, and must return a structured decision.

\begin{promptbox}
[Judge Model: Gemma 3 1B]

[Prompt Template]
You are an evaluation judge for LLM conformity experiments.
Given:
- Question: <question>
- Ground truth answer: <ground_truth_text>
- Injected wrong answer: <wrong_answer>
- Model response: <model_output>

Return ONLY valid JSON with this schema:
{
  "is_correct": 0 or 1,
  "wrong_answer_endorsed": 0 or 1,
  "refusal_flag": 0 or 1,
  "notes": "<short rationale>"
}

Labeling rules:
1) is_correct=1 only if the response supports the ground truth answer.
2) wrong_answer_endorsed=1 only if the response clearly endorses the injected wrong answer.
3) refusal_flag=1 for abstentions/refusals/non-answers.
4) Output JSON only, no extra text.
\end{promptbox}

The judge model is explicitly fixed to Gemma 3 1B. Its output schema contains four fields: is\_correct, wrong\_answer\_endorsed, refusal\_flag, and notes. Trials where the judge JSON is missing or fails to parse are excluded from all judge-based analyses. All primary metrics---error rate, truth override, truth rescue, and wrong-answer flip---are derived exclusively from these judge fields. Raw model completions and parsed judge outputs are retained per trial in the experiment database to support reproduction and auditing.
