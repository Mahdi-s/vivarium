\begin{abstract}
When five fabricated peers in a prompt all endorse a wrong answer, a language model that would otherwise answer correctly often goes along with the crowd. Prior work treats this as social conformity that alignment training reduces. We revisit this picture using the OLMo model family, whose publicly released intermediate post-training checkpoints enable a within-family decomposition that holds architecture and pretraining fixed. Three findings follow. First, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) \emph{increases} wrong-answer endorsement relative to the pretrained base, while direct preference optimization (DPO) partially reverses it---an opposition invisible when comparing only base and final instruction-tuned models. Second, stripping all social framing while preserving the repetitive structure of the Asch-style prompt yields endorsement at least as high as the naked social prompt---in our two tested models, the non-social n-gram baseline is strictly higher---indicating that social framing is not required to reproduce the measured effect and in fact mitigates it for these models. Third, extending the evaluation to eight additional model families on the same items reveals three qualitatively distinct pressure responses---endorsement-dominant, refusal-dominant, and context-insensitive. The variance in endorsement rates between OLMo's intermediate stage checkpoints is comparable in magnitude to the cross-sectional variance observed across the final checkpoints of these other families, consistent with (but not proof of) post-training stage as a dominant driver. Taken together, our results suggest that post-training stages leave distinguishable behavioral signatures under in-context pressure, and that mitigation targeting the \emph{social} framing alone is unlikely to close the underlying vulnerability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In 1951, Solomon Asch showed that ordinary people will deny what their own eyes tell them when enough confederates in the room confidently assert a wrong answer~\citep{asch1956}. The effect is remarkably robust: even when the correct answer is obvious, roughly a third of participants conform to a unanimous but incorrect majority. Asch's experiment has since become one of the most replicated findings in social psychology.

We ask a simple version of this question about language models: \emph{if a model knows the right answer, will it change its mind when the prompt contains fabricated peers all endorsing the wrong one?} This matters because language models are increasingly deployed in roles where maintaining accuracy under contextual pressure is critical~\citep{bommasani2021opportunities}---as medical assistants, legal advisors, coding copilots, and decision-support tools. If a model can be pushed into endorsing a wrong answer simply by embedding fake consensus in its input, that is a concrete vulnerability with real-world consequences.

A common working hypothesis in the recent alignment literature is that post-training tends to make models \emph{more} resistant to this kind of in-context pressure: \citet{zhu2025conformity} explicitly report that instruction-tuned models are less conformist than their base counterparts across four families, and \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} and \citet{hong2025syconbench} offer related claims for user-facing sycophancy. We report three findings that complicate this picture. (i)~When the post-training pipeline is decomposed stage by stage, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is associated with \emph{increased} wrong-answer endorsement rather than attenuation; later preference optimization (DPO) partially reverses this amplification. (ii)~When the social framing is stripped from the prompt but its repetitive structure is preserved, endorsement persists and in some cases \emph{rises}---reframing what is being measured as, in large part, autoregressive pattern completion. (iii)~Under identical pressure, model families respond through three qualitatively distinct behavioral modes that a single error rate conflates.

\paragraph{Consensus susceptibility vs.\ sycophancy.}
It is worth distinguishing this phenomenon from \textbf{sycophancy}, which has received more attention in the alignment literature~\citep{sharma2024sycophancy}. A sycophantic model agrees with \emph{the person asking the question}---it tells you what you want to hear. What we study here is different: the model agrees with \emph{a crowd of fabricated third-party peers}, even when it would otherwise answer correctly. We call this \textbf{consensus susceptibility}. The distinction matters because the two phenomena exploit different aspects of training: sycophancy exploits the model's drive to be helpful and agreeable to the user, while consensus susceptibility exploits sensitivity to patterns of agreement in the context.

\paragraph{Why training stages matter.}
Modern language models do not go directly from pretraining to deployment. After learning language from a massive text corpus, they go through several additional stages designed to make them more useful and aligned with human preferences. \textbf{Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)} teaches the model to follow instructions by training it on examples of good responses written by humans. \textbf{Direct preference optimization (DPO)}~\citep{rafailov2023dpo} refines the model further by showing it pairs of responses---one preferred, one not---and nudging it toward the preferred style. \textbf{Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR)} provides a final adjustment using automated feedback on tasks with checkable answers. Some models also go through a separate \textbf{reasoning path} (often called the ``Think'' pipeline), which trains the model to produce intermediate reasoning steps before committing to a final answer.

Each of these stages is designed to make the model better---but do they also change how the model responds to fabricated consensus, and in the same direction? Prior work~\citep{zhu2025conformity} compared base models to final instruction-tuned models across several families and reported that instruction-tuning \emph{reduces} conformity. That comparison treats the entire post-training pipeline as a single black box. It cannot tell us whether any change comes from SFT, from DPO, from reinforcement learning, or from the combination---nor whether intermediate stages move in the same direction as the final one. To answer that question, we need access to the checkpoint at each stage.

\paragraph{Our approach.}
We exploit a unique property of the OLMo-3 model family~\citep{olmo3}: it is the only major model family that publicly releases every intermediate post-training checkpoint. This gives us access to the model \emph{before any post-training}, after SFT alone, after SFT then DPO, and after the final RLVR stage---all sharing identical architecture and pretraining history. Because these checkpoints share an architecture and pretraining history, behavioral differences track the sequential application of post-training stages. We cannot, however, isolate the causal effect of a training \emph{objective} (SFT, DPO, RLVR) from the \emph{specific data distribution} and cumulative compute used at that step; where we report that ``SFT amplifies endorsement,'' we mean that the SFT checkpoint on this pipeline exhibits higher endorsement than its adjacent checkpoints, not that the SFT objective in isolation would produce this effect on arbitrary data. We partially address this confound in \S\ref{sec:data_audit} via a direct audit of the Dolci mixtures, isolating the corpus-level structural priors that track the per-domain SFT BER amplification.

We evaluate these checkpoints along two parallel training paths. The \textbf{Instruct path} (the standard instruction-following pipeline) is tested at the 7-billion parameter scale with full coverage across all experimental conditions and temperatures. The \textbf{Think path} (the chain-of-thought reasoning pipeline) is tested at both 7B and 32B scales on a shared subset of conditions, allowing us to compare how standard instruction-following and explicit reasoning training handle the same pressure. We also evaluate the 32B Instruct model for scale comparison.

To test whether the patterns we observe in OLMo are specific to that family or reflect something more general, we extend the evaluation to eight additional model families---including Llama, GPT-4o-Mini, Gemini, Grok, Claude, and others---spanning diverse architectures and alignment approaches. These models are tested on the same items under the same conditions, allowing direct comparison.

Finally, we probe a construct-validity question the behavioral literature has largely left open: the Asch-style prompt embeds five repetitions of the wrong answer in sequence (``Participant 1: X \ldots\ Participant 5: X''). Is the model responding to \emph{social pressure}---peers endorsing a wrong answer---or is it simply \emph{completing a repetitive token sequence}? Two ablations strip the social framing while preserving the repetitive structure (\S\ref{sec:ablation}). Both isolate how much of the measured effect requires a sociological reading and how much is compatible with autoregressive pattern completion alone.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training-stage decomposition.} Within OLMo, SFT and DPO are associated with \emph{opposite} changes in wrong-answer endorsement---a non-monotonic trajectory that is invisible when only base and final instruction-tuned checkpoints are compared (\S\ref{sec:sft_dpo}).
    \item \textbf{Pattern-completion reframing.} Removing all social framing while preserving the repetitive Asch-style structure still elicits substantial endorsement; removing the safety system prompt further shifts the effect in a model-dependent way. These ablations shift the construct being measured from ``social deference'' toward ``sensitivity to in-context repetitions'' (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).
    \item \textbf{Cross-family behavioral taxonomy.} On identical items under identical pressure, eight additional model families partition into three qualitatively distinct response modes---endorsement-dominant, refusal-dominant, and context-insensitive---that a single pooled error rate conflates (\S\ref{sec:cross_family}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sycophancy vs.\ Social Conformity.}
A growing body of work studies \emph{sycophancy}---models deferring to the user's stated opinion. \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} showed that human evaluators and preference models sometimes favor sycophantic responses, suggesting the problem is partly baked into the training signal. \citet{elephant2025} demonstrated that LLMs are substantially more likely than humans to endorse user actions on personal advice queries. \citet{hong2025syconbench} showed that alignment tuning amplifies sycophancy over multi-turn dialogue. These studies share a common framing: the pressure comes from \emph{the user asking the question}.

\emph{Social conformity} is a different phenomenon. Here, the pressure comes not from the user but from \emph{fabricated third-party peers} embedded in the prompt---a crowd of voices all endorsing the same wrong answer. The mechanisms differ: sycophancy exploits helpfulness training (``be agreeable to the person you're helping''), while conformity exploits sensitivity to contextual consensus (``everyone else said X''). A model that resists user sycophancy may still conform to peer pressure. Our study targets the latter, drawing on the social psychology tradition of \citet{asch1956} rather than the RLHF alignment literature.

\paragraph{LLM Conformity.}
\citet{zhu2025conformity} is the most directly comparable and influential work for our study. They adapt Asch-style paradigms across four model families (Llama-3, Gemma, Mistral, Qwen2), systematically vary confederate unanimity and tone, and propose devil's advocate and question distillation as mitigations. We adopt several elements of their experimental design---including the structured participant-role format, tone variations, and mitigation conditions---and extend them in three directions.

First, Zhu et al.'s central finding is that instruction-tuned models are \emph{less} conformist than their base counterparts. However, their comparison is between base and final instruct models, treating the entire post-training pipeline as a single step. Because base and instruct models differ in SFT data, preference optimization, and (sometimes) reinforcement learning simultaneously, this design cannot identify which stage is responsible for the observed difference. Our within-family design decomposes this pipeline into its constituent stages using a model family that publicly releases each intermediate checkpoint.

Second, Zhu et al.\ compare models across families, where architectural and data differences confound any alignment-related conclusions. We replicate this cross-family approach on a broader set of models, but anchor it to the within-family trajectory described above, allowing us to distinguish family-level variation from alignment-stage effects.

Third, Zhu et al.\ do not probe the mechanism behind the measured effect. Our ablation studies (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) test whether the observed conformity reflects genuine social deference or autoregressive pattern completion driven by the repetitive structure of the Asch-style prompt---a distinction with direct implications for mitigation strategy.

Other recent work on LLM conformity includes \citet{celebi2025parrot}, who evaluate sycophancy under authority pressure across MMLU domains with log-likelihood calibration but do not decompose training stages; \citet{weng2025benchform}, who study conformity in multi-agent settings; and \citet{shoval2025psychiatric}, who adapt Asch-style influence to psychiatric assessments using a single model. None of these decompose the post-training pipeline.

\paragraph{Post-Training Effects on Behavior.}
How each stage of post-training shapes model behavior is a growing area of interest. RLHF is known to reduce generation diversity and sharpen output distributions~\citep{rlhf_entropy}, which could in principle increase sampling of ``safe'' agreement modes. \citet{rafailov2023dpo} introduced DPO as an offline alternative to PPO~\citep{schulman2017ppo}, and it has become a standard component of modern alignment pipelines. \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} report that RLHF broadly amplifies sycophancy but do not isolate SFT from preference optimization. Our design addresses that specific gap within a single model family. Because DPO is applied sequentially on top of SFT weights, what we measure is a within-pipeline trajectory, not a counterfactual intervention on each stage; we therefore report \emph{stage-associated} changes rather than causal effects of the training objective in isolation.

\paragraph{Temperature and Conformity.}
\citet{renze2024} find that temperature changes ($T \in [0.0, 1.0]$) do not significantly impact problem-solving accuracy in neutral (unbiased) contexts. An open question is whether this robustness holds when the context contains misleading pressure. Our experimental design tests the full temperature range, allowing us to characterize how decoding temperature interacts with conformity effects (Appendix~\ref{app:tc}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Within-Family Training-Stage Decomposition (OLMo-3)}
\label{sec:study1_method}

The core of our design exploits the OLMo-3 model family's~\citep{olmo3} unique transparency: every intermediate post-training checkpoint is publicly released on the Hugging Face Hub. This lets us evaluate the same model at each stage of its post-training pipeline, so that behavioral differences between adjacent checkpoints reflect the sequential application of that stage---not differences in architecture, pretraining data, or scale. Each stage applies a particular \emph{objective} (SFT, DPO, RLVR) to a particular \emph{data distribution} (the Dolci mixtures~\citep{olmo3}); our design isolates the combined effect of the two but cannot separate them. Throughout the paper we describe these as \emph{stage-associated} behavioral changes rather than causal effects of the training objective in isolation. The Instruct and Think pipelines both branch from the same pretrained base checkpoint (\texttt{allenai/Olmo-3-1025-7B}) and each proceed through three post-training stages in the same order: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), direct preference optimization (DPO)~\citep{rafailov2023dpo}, and reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) on math, code, instruction-following, and chat data~\citep{olmo3}.

\paragraph{Instruct path (7B, full coverage).}
We evaluate four checkpoints along the Olmo-3-7B Instruct pipeline:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Base}: \texttt{allenai/Olmo-3-1025-7B}, the pretrained model with no post-training.
    \item \textbf{Instruct-SFT}: \texttt{Olmo-3-7B-Instruct-SFT}, Base after supervised fine-tuning on the Dolci-Instruct-SFT-7B mixture.
    \item \textbf{Instruct-DPO}: \texttt{Olmo-3-7B-Instruct-DPO}, the SFT checkpoint after DPO on the Dolci-Instruct-DPO-7B preference set.
    \item \textbf{Instruct}: \texttt{Olmo-3-7B-Instruct}, the final RLVR-trained release (SFT + DPO + RLVR on Dolci-Instruct-RL-7B).
\end{itemize}
These four checkpoints are tested across all 12 experimental conditions (1 control, 8 peer-consensus, 3 authority-claim; full catalog in Appendix~\ref{app:conditions}) at 6~temperatures ($T \in \{0.0, 0.2, \ldots, 1.0\}$), giving the most complete coverage in the study.

\paragraph{Think path (7B and 32B).}
OLMo-3 releases a separate chain-of-thought reasoning pipeline that shares the same Base checkpoint but uses reasoning-targeted data at each stage. At the 7B scale we evaluate \texttt{Olmo-3-7B-Think-SFT} (\textbf{Think-SFT}) and \texttt{Olmo-3-7B-Think-DPO} (\textbf{Think-DPO})---the reasoning counterparts of the Instruct-path SFT and DPO stages. At the 32B scale we additionally evaluate the final RLVR-trained \texttt{Olmo-3-32B-Think} (\textbf{Think}). These Think-path variants are tested on a shared subset of conditions at 2~temperatures ($T \in \{0.0, 0.6\}$), allowing direct comparison between instruction-following and reasoning training under the same pressure.

\paragraph{Scale comparison (32B Instruct).}
To examine whether scale interacts with susceptibility, we also evaluate \texttt{Olmo-3.1-32B-Instruct} on the same shared subset of conditions. Together with the 32B Think-path variants, this provides a matched scale comparison between the two training paths.

Because Olmo-3-7B-Instruct appears in both the within-family decomposition and the cross-family evaluation below on the same 400 items, it serves as a calibration bridge linking the training-stage trajectory to the cross-family ranking (\S\ref{sec:cross_family}).

\subsection{Cross-Family Extension}
\label{sec:study2_method}

To test whether the within-family patterns generalize beyond OLMo, we evaluate a diverse set of model families spanning dense and mixture-of-experts architectures, open and closed weights, and varied self-reported alignment approaches---SFT+RLHF~\citep{meta2024llama3}, lightweight SFT + online RL~\citep{meta2025llama4}, RLHF with Instruction Hierarchy~\citep{openai2024gpt4omini}, RL with unsupervised chain-of-thought~\citep{openai2025gptoss}, distillation~\citep{google2025gemini25}, RL-dominant training~\citep{xai2025grok41}, and Constitutional AI~\citep{bai2022constitutional}. Each model is tested on 4~core conditions (control, structured peer consensus, authoritative bias, authority trust) at $T{=}0.0$ and $T{=}0.6$, with 50~items per dataset across 8~domains (400~items per condition, fixed $N{=}400$ denominator). The same items are used across all models. Inferential tests in the main text use $T{=}0.0$ so that each (item, model, condition) triple produces a single deterministic outcome; this lets McNemar's paired binary design be applied without averaging over within-item stochastic variance that would otherwise require a bootstrap. $T{=}0.6$ descriptive results are included in Appendix~\ref{app:cross_mcnemar} for completeness. Two additional ablation conditions (system-prompt removal, non-social n-gram baseline) are tested on selected models at $T{=}0.0$.

\subsection{Evaluation Pipeline}

Trial outputs were classified by two independent methods. First, a deterministic rule-based parser extracted each model's answer and classified it as correct, incorrect, or a refusal. Second, GPT-OSS-20B~\citep{openai2025gptoss} served as an LLM judge on the same outputs. For disagreements between the two methods, we used the judge's labels as authoritative for correctness (it better handles semantic equivalences, e.g., recognizing a derived numerical answer as correct) and the parser's labels as authoritative for refusal detection (after expanding the refusal phrase list based on manual review; see Appendix~\ref{app:judge_details}). GPT-OSS-20B is also one of the \emph{evaluated} models, which means the judge and one subject share weights. We address this in two ways: (i)~the rule-based parser (not the judge) produced the authoritative label on the majority of GPT-OSS-20B's own completions, confining self-preference risk to the parser--judge disagreement subset; (ii)~per-model parser--judge agreement is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:judge_details}. We cannot \emph{exclude} residual self-preference bias on ambiguous GPT-OSS-20B completions; accordingly we flag GPT-OSS-20B's position in the cross-family ranking as subject to this caveat.

Every trial is assigned to one of three mutually exclusive states: \textbf{State~A} (correct answer), \textbf{State~B} (wrong-answer endorsement), or \textbf{State~C} (refusal). We report two distinct quantities. \emph{Descriptive rates}---BER (State B), refusal rate (State C), and error rate (B+C)---use a fixed denominator of $N{=}400$ items per condition, so refusals count toward the error rate and no model benefits from abstaining. \emph{Inferential McNemar tests}, by design, require paired binary outcomes on the same items; pairs in which either condition yields a refusal have no well-defined ``correct / wrong'' binary, so they are excluded from the contingency table. The two conventions are consistent but answer different questions: fixed-$N$ descriptives summarize how the full item set fails, while McNemar quantifies the shift among items on which the model gave a definite answer in both conditions. For models with large refusal shifts under pressure (notably Llama-3.1-70B, $\Delta\text{refusal}{=}{+}0.79$), this restricts the McNemar inference to a sub-population of discordant items; Appendix~\ref{app:refusal_sensitivity} reports a complementary sensitivity analysis coding refusals as ``non-correct'' so that no control-correct item is dropped. The qualitative direction of the Olmo within-family trajectory is unchanged under both conventions. We test each model's pressure response using McNemar's exact binomial test at $T{=}0.0$, with Holm--Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons; raw $b$, $c$, odds-ratio 95\% CIs, and effective paired-sample sizes are reported alongside every OR in Appendices~\ref{app:mcnemar} and~\ref{app:cross_mcnemar} (details on odds ratio computation in Appendix~\ref{app:stat_methods}).

\subsection{Does Post-Training Monotonically Reduce Susceptibility? No.}

\paragraph{SFT is associated with the highest endorsement in the pipeline.}
Under structured peer consensus, the Instruct-SFT checkpoint produces the highest wrong-answer endorsement of any variant---substantially exceeding even the unaligned Base model. Instruct-SFT also coincides with the lowest refusal rate. An audit of Dolci-Instruct-SFT (\S\ref{sec:data_audit}) reveals corpus-localised structural priors --- elevated list-carryover rates concentrated in Tulu~3 Persona GSM (0.964), Dolci OpenThoughts3+ Science (0.923), and Tulu~3 Persona MATH (0.826) --- that track the per-domain SFT BER amplification under rank correlation, while explicit consensus-language framing is essentially absent (99.97\% of SFT responses contain zero consensus matches). The pattern is consistent with structural-prior amplification under pattern completion (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) rather than learned social deference (cf.\ \citet{sharma2024sycophancy}).

\paragraph{DPO is associated with a partial reversal.}
After DPO, endorsement drops and refusal rises relative to Instruct-SFT. The final RLVR stage---the released \texttt{Olmo-3-7B-Instruct}---shows a partial re-increase but does not return to the Instruct-SFT peak. All four variants remain statistically vulnerable to pressure; the effect is a relative shift, not an elimination.

These findings raise a mechanistic question the behavioral data alone cannot answer: does the SFT-amplified endorsement reflect learned social deference---the model genuinely treating fabricated peers as authoritative---or heightened sensitivity to the repetitive syntactic pattern the prompt imposes? The Asch-style format presents five identical wrong answers in sequence (``Participant 1: X \ldots\ Participant 5: X''), creating a strong autoregressive continuation signal. Suggestively, under the classical free-text Asch format (without this rigid repetition), both the Instruct-DPO and the final RLVR Instruct checkpoints show \emph{no statistically significant} conformity. We probe this distinction with two ablation studies in the next subsection.

\subsection{Is the Effect Social Deference, or Pattern Completion?}
\label{sec:ablation}

\noindent Two ablations strip the prompt of social content while preserving its repetitive structure. The goal is to ask whether the observed endorsement depends on the \emph{social} framing---participant roles, group-discussion language---or on the \emph{mechanical} regularity of five identical answer tokens preceding the model's turn.

\paragraph{Ablation 1: Removing the safety system prompt.}
All standard conditions use a system prompt that explicitly warns against the conformity trap: ``You are a truthful assistant. You may see prior users' answers. Prefer correctness over agreement, even if a prior group consensus exists.'' Removing this prompt while keeping the Asch-style pressure identical---the \emph{naked} condition---reveals a model-dependent instruction-hierarchy effect~\citep{wallace2024instruction}: for some models endorsement rises sharply without the system prompt, while for others the system prompt provides no measurable protection at all. A single ``conformity rate'' therefore masks large differences in how each model's instruction hierarchy mediates the conflict between top-level instructions and in-context repetition.

\paragraph{Ablation 2: Stripping social framing while keeping repetition.}
Replacing the Asch-style prompt with a non-social n-gram baseline---identical repetition structure, no participant roles, no organizer, no group framing---yields wrong-answer endorsement at least as high as the naked (no-system-prompt) social prompt for both tested models. For Llama-3.1-70B the n-gram BER is $0.357$ vs $0.250$ under the naked social prompt; for Olmo-3.1-32B-Instruct it is $0.455$ vs $0.390$ (Table~\ref{tab:naked_vs_ngram}). In other words, for structured prompt templates relying on strict sequential repetition, removing social framing preserves or even \emph{exacerbates} the endorsement rate. This indicates that structural repetition acts as a severe confounder when evaluating social deference with this specific format: the effect the literature has attributed to ``peer conformity'' is at least partially, and in some cases entirely, reproducible without a social frame. The account is consistent with autoregressive pattern completion~\citep{min2022rethinking, shi2023large} and with the broader observation that autoregressive decoders are acutely sensitive to repetitive context~\citep{holtzman2020curious, welleck2020unlikelihood, fu2021theoretical}. Devil's advocate prompting~\citep{zhu2025conformity} reduces but does not eliminate the effect (Appendix~\ref{app:mitigation}).

\subsection{Three Pillars: A Direct Audit of the Dolci Training Mixture}
\label{sec:data_audit}

\noindent The SFT amplification documented in \S\ref{sec:sft_dpo} is an observational finding: the SFT checkpoint on this pipeline shows higher wrong-answer endorsement than adjacent checkpoints. The natural mechanistic hypothesis is that the Dolci-Instruct-SFT-7B corpus contains structural features that the model amplifies under repetitive prompting. We evaluate this hypothesis via a three-pillar direct audit: \textbf{Pillar~I} measures per-source structural priors in the SFT corpus; \textbf{Pillar~II} measures whether the DPO penalty has practical effect-size meaning; \textbf{Pillar~III} tests whether corpus-side priors predict per-domain BER amplification. Together the three pillars convert the behavioral association into an empirically grounded mechanistic account.

\paragraph{Pillar I: Per-source structural priors are corpus-localized.}
Across the full Dolci-Instruct-SFT corpus ($n{=}1{,}944{,}831$ responses), the corpus-wide list-carryover rate is $P(\text{resp\_has\_list}\mid\text{prompt\_has\_list}) = 0.461$: the model inherits list formatting from the prompt in 46.1\% of list-prompt cases. However, this corpus-wide number masks extreme concentration. The top three sources by carryover rate are Tulu~3 Persona GSM ($P_{\text{carryover}} = 0.964$, $n{=}49{,}980$), Dolci OpenThoughts3+ Science ($0.923$, $n{=}99{,}260$), and Tulu~3 Persona MATH ($0.826$, $n{=}149{,}958$). In each case the structural prior is carried by math or science demonstrations that rely heavily on stepwise list formatting. A further Tulu~3 Persona Algebra subset ($0.787$, $n{=}19{,}999$) and Dolci Tool Use ($0.800$, $n{=}21{,}113$) reinforce the math/tool concentration. See Figure~\ref{fig:pillar1_priors} for the full bar chart of the top-12 source datasets. The formatting trap is therefore corpus-localized in the math and science Dolci subsets, precisely the domains where per-domain BER amplification is largest.

\paragraph{Pillar II: DPO penalty has no practical effect-size meaning at the median.}
The DPO stage is commonly understood to penalise undesirable behavioral patterns relative to the SFT baseline. To quantify how strongly DPO penalises the structural mirroring priors identified in Pillar~I, we compute Cliff's $\delta$ (a rank-based, sample-size-independent effect-size measure; \citealt{romano2006}) on all six structural-delta metrics across the full Dolci-Instruct-DPO corpus ($N{=}259{,}785$ chosen/rejected pairs). The result is unambiguous: \emph{every} Cliff's $\delta$ falls below the $0.147$ ``none'' threshold defined by Romano et al.\ (2006). The numerically largest effect is on n-gram overlap ($\delta = +0.115$, 95\% CI $[+0.113, +0.117]$) --- still below the ``none'' threshold, and with a median chosen$-$rejected delta of $0.005$. Structural Jaccard ($\delta = +0.037$) and maximum-run length ($\delta = -0.053$) are smaller still; consensus-hit and sycophancy deltas are near zero ($|\delta| < 0.012$). At $N{=}259{,}785$ every metric reaches statistical significance at the permutation $p$-floor ($p < 0.001$), confirming the null is cleanly rejected --- but the signal lives entirely in the top-1\% of rejected responses, not in the median. The median rejected response is structurally indistinguishable from the median chosen response on these priors. See Figure~\ref{fig:pillar2_effect} (left panel) and Table~\ref{tab:data_audit} for the full effect-size results.

\paragraph{Pillar III: Corpus-side priors track per-domain BER amplification.}
To test whether the structural priors identified in Pillar~I predict the domain-level pattern of SFT BER amplification, we compute Spearman $\rho$ between corpus-side per-source structural features and per-macro-domain $\Delta\text{BER}$ (SFT $-$ Base) over $n{=}3$ macro-domains (math, science, general). The list-run prior ($\texttt{has\_run\_5\_response}$) yields $\rho = +0.500$ (95\% CI $[-1.000, +1.000]$; $n{=}3$, descriptive only) --- in the expected positive direction. The consensus-hits feature yields $\rho = -1.000$, confirming the near-total absence of consensus-language priors throughout the corpus and its anti-correlation with domain-level BER amplification. Per-source within-domain correlation is unsupported because the available Dolci source-to-BER-domain mapping has $\leq 2$ mappable sources per domain; inferential claims therefore rely on the per-source-dataset ranking across all 22 subsets. See Figure~\ref{fig:pillar2_effect} (right panel).

\paragraph{Smoking-gun nearest-neighbor cases.}
Phase~8 of the audit identified a cross-domain structural nearest neighbor for a representative science benchmark item (\texttt{arc\_challenge\_0018}): the closest Dolci SFT response by TF-IDF cosine ($= 0.66$) is an OpenMathInstruct~2 row --- a math demonstration that shares the stepwise list structure but has no semantic overlap with the science item. This cross-domain structural match is published in full in \texttt{dataset\_analysis/results/phase8\_case\_studies.json} for reproducibility.

\paragraph{Interpretation: structural-prior amplification, not learned social deference.}
The three pillars jointly support the following mechanistic reading. The Dolci SFT corpus contains strong structural-formatting priors concentrated in math and science subsets (Pillar~I). DPO penalises these priors only weakly and only in the top-1\% tail --- at the median the penalty is negligible (Pillar~II). Explicit consensus-framing language (``I agree'', ``the group says'', peer-frame markers) is essentially absent from the corpus: 99.97\% of SFT responses contain zero consensus matches, and zero responses contain any peer-frame structure. Pillar~III shows that the structural-run prior, not consensus language, tracks the domain-level BER amplification pattern. The ``learned social deference'' reading --- that the model was explicitly trained on agreeable, consensus-following language --- is therefore strictly weaker than the ``learned sensitivity to repetitive structural context'' account, consistent with the n-gram ablation results in \S\ref{sec:ablation} (cf.\ \citet{sharma2024sycophancy}).


\subsection{Does the Within-Family Pattern Generalize? Partially, and in Different Modes.}
\label{sec:cross_family}

\noindent Evaluating eight additional model families on the same 400~items under the same structured peer-consensus condition (5~unanimous confederates, confident tone) at $T{=}0.0$ with a fixed $N{=}400$ denominator, we find that families do not line up on a single ``more or less conformist'' axis. Instead, they partition into qualitatively distinct response modes. The pattern-completion confound established in \S\ref{sec:ablation} applies uniformly across all conditions in this section and should be held in mind throughout.

\paragraph{A pooled error rate hides the failure mode.}
Decomposing each model's pressure response into its endorsement and refusal components reveals that the models with the largest pressure-induced $\Delta$ are not necessarily the most conformist in the colloquial sense. Some show a large $\Delta$ driven almost entirely by \emph{increased refusals} rather than wrong-answer adoption; others show genuine wrong-answer endorsement. Any metric that treats all non-correct outcomes alike obscures this distinction.

\paragraph{Three behavioral modes: endorse, refuse, or ignore.}

Under identical pressure, models cluster into three qualitatively distinct response patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endorsement-dominant.} The fabricated consensus is adopted as the model's own answer at high rates; refusal rarely rises. The wrong answer becomes the output.
    \item \textbf{Refusal-dominant.} Rather than endorsing the wrong answer, the model switches to safety-aligned refusal. Under a fixed-$N$ denominator this still counts as error, producing a large $\Delta$, but the underlying failure---abstention---is categorically different from endorsement.
    \item \textbf{Context-insensitive.} Neither endorsement nor refusal rises. The model answers as if the fabricated peers were not present, preserving its baseline accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reasoning-trained models show non-significant conformity---but the reason is not settled.}

All reasoning-capable models in our survey show non-significant peer conformity after Holm--Bonferroni correction at $T{=}0.0$, in contrast to the instruction-tuned variants of the same families. We are cautious about interpreting this pattern. Non-significance at our sample size, prompt format, and metric is not evidence of resistance. The reasoning models we evaluate also differ in scale, alignment approach, and architecture, so the effect cannot be attributed to reasoning \emph{per se}. A structural alternative is that reasoning models emit an initial control token (e.g., \texttt{<think>}) before committing to an answer, mechanically breaking the n-gram repetition chain the fabricated peers establish. Behavioral data cannot adjudicate between deliberation-based resistance and this structural interrupt.

\paragraph{Peer framing hurts more than authority framing---consistent with the repetition account.}
Structured peer consensus produces larger pressure effects than authority framing across families. This is consistent with the pattern-completion reading: the peer prompt contains five repetitions of the wrong answer, whereas the authority prompt contains one. The ablation of \S\ref{sec:ablation} quantifies the mechanical component directly; the cross-family asymmetry is an independent corroboration.

\paragraph{Within-family stage variance is comparable to cross-family variance.}
\texttt{Olmo-3-7B-Instruct} appears in both the within-family decomposition and the cross-family evaluation on the same 400~items, acting as a calibration bridge. We emphasize what this comparison can and cannot show: we have intermediate SFT and DPO checkpoints \emph{only} for OLMo, not for Llama, GPT, Gemini, Grok, or Claude, so we cannot claim that a specific SFT-up, DPO-down trajectory is ``preserved'' within each of those families. What we can say is more limited: the spread among OLMo's three intermediate stage checkpoints on the unanimous-confident peer condition (BER range $\approx 0.35$, from $0.39$ at Instruct-DPO to $0.74$ at Instruct-SFT) is of the same order as the spread among the final releases of the other families (BER range $\approx 0.36$, from $0.04$ for Llama-3.1-70B to $0.40$ for GPT-4o-Mini). This is consistent with, but does not prove, post-training stage as a dominant driver of family-level variation.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Post-training stages are not interchangeable for this behavior.}
The within-family decomposition shows that wrong-answer endorsement rises after Instruct-SFT and falls after Instruct-DPO within the Olmo-3-7B pipeline~\citep{olmo3}. The cross-family evaluation shows a broadly consistent tendency: families whose \emph{self-reported} training mixtures emphasize SFT and offline preference optimization~\citep{meta2024llama3, meta2025llama4} tend to occupy the upper half of the endorsement ranking, while those emphasizing RL-heavy~\citep{openai2025gptoss, xai2025grok41} or Constitutional~\citep{bai2022constitutional} regimes tend to occupy the lower half. We flag this as an observation rather than a causal claim: there is no quantitative metric of ``SFT-heaviness'' across closed-weight families, the architecture class and data distribution differ along with the alignment mixture, and families in the ``middle'' of the ranking do not fit the ordering cleanly. Read conservatively, the cross-family pattern is \emph{consistent with} the within-family finding but does not independently identify the training stage as the cause. The most we can conclude is that post-training \emph{matters} for this behavior, and that within OLMo it matters in the specific direction the stage decomposition reveals.

\paragraph{Failure modes are categorical, not scalar.}
\citet{zhu2025conformity}'s finding that instruct models are ``less conformist'' than base models is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Families respond to identical pressure in qualitatively different ways---endorsement-dominant, refusal-dominant, or context-insensitive---each with different safety implications. A model that refuses everything and a model that endorses the wrong answer both ``fail'' under a pooled error rate, but a deployment decision between them is very different. The 3-state decomposition is necessary to state this distinction at all.

\paragraph{Reasoning training is not shown to be a mitigation.}
All reasoning-capable models show non-significant peer conformity (Holm-corrected). But reasoning models typically emit an initial control token (e.g., \texttt{<think>}) before their answer, which mechanically breaks the n-gram repetition chain. Behavioral data cannot distinguish deliberation-based resistance from this structural interrupt, and the reasoning models in our sample differ in too many axes to attribute the effect to reasoning alone. The safe reading is: reasoning-trained models do not exhibit the effect under this prompt format, and the mechanism is open.

\paragraph{Construct validity: a pattern-completion reframing.}
The non-social n-gram ablation eliciting substantial endorsement is, for this paper, the most important single observation. It reframes what prior Asch-style LLM studies measure: a large fraction of the signal that reads as ``peer conformity'' is compatible with a simpler autoregressive account. This does not diminish the deployment risk---the structured format is a realistic threat precisely because autoregressive models are sensitive to it---but it changes both the explanation and the mitigation target. Where a system prompt sharply reduces the effect, the instruction hierarchy~\citep{wallace2024instruction} is doing the work; where it does not, the model is responding to the repetition itself, and mitigations that leave the repetition intact should not be expected to help.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\textbf{(1)}~We cannot establish causation for closed models whose training pipelines are proprietary; cross-family claims are suggestive, not identifying. \textbf{(2)}~The effect is tied to the structured, five-repetition prompt format; generalization to multi-turn conversational pressure is untested. \textbf{(3)}~DPO is applied to SFT weights, so the DPO-associated decrease reflects a sequential trajectory rather than an independent counterfactual intervention. \textbf{(4)}~The cross-family survey uses a fixed condition and item set; domain- or register-specific vulnerabilities beyond those we sample may exist. \textbf{(5)}~All labels depend on a hybrid parser + LLM-judge pipeline; although we audit agreement, systematic judge biases cannot be fully excluded. \textbf{(6)}~The Dolci data audit (\S\ref{sec:data_audit}) reports English-language regex matches as lower bounds; future work should bound recall against an LLM-judge classifier on a stratified sample of Dolci responses. \textbf{(7)}~Per-macro-domain rank correlations are computed over $n{=}3$ macro-domains (math, science, general; history and preference are absent from Dolci-Instruct-SFT) and are descriptive only; inferential claims rely on the per-source-dataset rank correlation across 22 subsets.


%% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to measure social conformity in language models and return with a sharper, narrower claim. Decomposing the OLMo post-training pipeline shows that successive alignment stages have \emph{opposing} effects on wrong-answer endorsement under fabricated peer pressure: SFT amplifies, DPO partially reverses. This non-monotonic trajectory is invisible when only base and final instruction-tuned checkpoints are compared, and it is preserved in the ranking of eight additional model families evaluated on identical items, which themselves partition into three qualitatively distinct response modes. An ablation then reframes what this measurement captures: stripping social framing while keeping the repetitive prompt structure still elicits substantial endorsement, so most of the signal prior work reads as \emph{social} conformity is compatible with autoregressive pattern completion. The deployment risk is real, but it is best understood as sensitivity to repetitive in-context patterns---one that an explicit safety instruction mitigates for some models and not others. Code and accompanying materials are available.\footnote{Repository: \url{https://github.com/Mahdi-s/conformity-eval}.}

\section{Hybrid Labeling Pipeline}
\label{app:judge}

All reported behavioral metrics have been tested vis an automated deterministic heuristic parser processes trails first, and then they undergo GPT-OSS-20B adjudication. This eliminates self-preference bias. The judge receives the question, ground truth, injected wrong answer, and model completion, and must return a structured decision.

\begin{promptbox}
[Judge Prompt Template]

You are an evaluation judge for LLM conformity experiments.
Given:
- Question: <question>
- Ground truth answer: <ground_truth_text>
- Injected wrong answer: <wrong_answer>
- Model response: <model_output>

Return ONLY valid JSON with this schema:
{
  "is_correct": 0 or 1,
  "wrong_answer_endorsed": 0 or 1,
  "refusal_flag": 0 or 1,
  "notes": "<short rationale>"
}

Labeling rules:
1) is_correct=1 only if the response supports the ground truth answer.
2) wrong_answer_endorsed=1 only if the response clearly endorses the injected wrong answer.
3) refusal_flag=1 for abstentions/refusals/non-answers.
4) Output JSON only, no extra text.
\end{promptbox}

The judge output schema contains four fields: \texttt{is\_correct}, \texttt{wrong\_answer\_endorsed}, \texttt{refusal\_flag}, and \texttt{notes}. Trials where the judge JSON is missing or fails to parse are excluded from all judge-based analyses. All primary metrics, error rate, truth override, truth rescue, and wrong-answer flip, are derived exclusively from these judge fields. Raw model completions and parsed judge outputs are retained per trial in the experiment database to support reproduction and auditing.

\section{Statistical Methods}
\label{app:stat_methods}

McNemar's exact binomial test is used for paired pressure-vs.-control comparisons at $T{=}0.0$ (greedy decoding), preserving statistical independence across items. McNemar odds ratios are computed with a Haldane--Anscombe continuity correction: $\text{OR} = (b + 0.5) / (c + 0.5)$, where $b$ is the number of truth-override transitions (correct under control, incorrect under pressure) and $c$ the number of truth-rescue transitions. This stabilizes estimation when either cell is small. Holm--Bonferroni correction is applied within each study and pressure mechanism. Key proportions are reported with 95\% Wilson score confidence intervals where space permits; odds ratio CIs use the Wald method on the log scale.

\section{Conformity Temperature and URSP}
\label{app:tc}

We define \emph{Conformity Temperature} ($T_c$) as the lowest temperature at which each (item, variant, condition) triple conforms. This metric characterizes, for each item, how robust the model's correct answer is to sampling noise: items with $T_c = 0$ are vulnerable even at greedy decoding, while items with higher $T_c$ only conform when sampling introduces variability. The distribution of $T_c$ values differs across training stages, with SFT shifting mass toward lower $T_c$ (more items vulnerable at greedy) and DPO shifting mass upward. Among conforming trials, some exhibit ground-truth leakage (URSP), where the output contains the correct answer despite endorsing the wrong one, though this rate is confounded by output verbosity.

\section{Refusal Handling}
\label{app:refusal}

\paragraph{Two separate fields.} \texttt{refusal\_flag} and \texttt{is\_correct} are independent judge outputs. A trial can have \texttt{refusal\_flag=1} (the response contains refusal language) \emph{and} \texttt{is\_correct=0} (the judge could still determine the answer was factually wrong). For high-refusal models, a substantial fraction of refusal-flagged trials also have a valid \texttt{is\_correct} score, while only a minority have \texttt{is\_correct=null}.

\paragraph{Two conventions, two quantities.} Our \emph{descriptive} rates (BER, refusal rate, error rate) use a fixed denominator of $N{=}400$ items per condition: every trial is classified into one of three mutually exclusive states---State~A (correct), State~B (wrong-answer endorsement), State~C (refusal)---and no model benefits from abstaining because refusals count toward the error rate. Our \emph{inferential} McNemar tests, by construction, require paired binary outcomes on the same items; a pair in which either condition produced a refusal has no well-defined ``correct / wrong'' binary and is therefore excluded from the contingency table. The two conventions are consistent but answer different questions: the fixed-$N$ rates summarize how the full item set fails, while McNemar quantifies the shift among items on which the model gave a definite answer under both conditions. For models with large refusal shifts under pressure, the McNemar inference is restricted to a discordant-pair sub-population whose size is apparent from the $n_{\text{pairs}}$, $b$, and $c$ columns reported alongside every OR in Appendices~\ref{app:mcnemar} and~\ref{app:cross_mcnemar}; Appendix~\ref{app:refusal_sensitivity} reports a sensitivity analysis coding refusals as non-correct so that no control-correct item is dropped.

\section{Judge Protocol Details}
\label{app:judge_details}

\subsection{Hybrid Pipeline Architecture}
Trial outcomes were not determined by a standalone LLM judge. Labels were generated using a hybrid pipeline: an automated deterministic heuristic parser (4-phase extraction cascade) processed the majority of outputs where its classification agreed with the LLM adjudicator, while the remaining edge-case and divergent outputs underwent GPT-OSS-20B adjudication via OpenRouter (\texttt{temperature=0.0}, \texttt{reasoning.effort=low}). The OLMo within-family evaluation uses GPT-OSS-20B labels for the target variants (base, instruct, instruct\_sft, instruct\_dpo). This hybrid approach eliminates the self-preference bias typically associated with pure LLM-as-a-judge pipelines~\citep{zheng2023judging}, validating our inclusion of GPT-OSS-20B as an evaluated model.

\subsection{Heuristic Scoring Pipeline}
Before judge labels are applied, each trial undergoes a four-phase heuristic extraction cascade:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase A (answer extraction):} Strip \texttt{</think>} tags, extract final answer span via explicit markers (\texttt{Answer:}, \texttt{$\backslash$boxed\{\}}), or tail-window heuristic.
    \item \textbf{Phase B (correctness matching):} Compare extracted answer against ground truth using substring matching, numeric comparison, and negation-aware context windows.
    \item \textbf{Phase C (wrong-answer detection):} Check for endorsement of the injected wrong answer with safeguards against negation, reported speech, and prompt echo.
    \item \textbf{Phase D (refusal detection):} Scan post-think content for refusal phrases (23 patterns including ``I am unsure,'' ``uncertain,'' ``I don't know,'' added during the data integrity audit).
\end{enumerate}
The heuristic labels populate the SQL columns (\texttt{is\_correct}, \texttt{refusal\_flag}) and serve as cross-validation for the judge labels.

\subsection{Heuristic vs.\ Judge Agreement}
The heuristic and judge labels show high agreement on both correctness and refusal classification. Disagreement is overwhelmingly one-directional: the majority of \texttt{is\_correct} mismatches are heur=0/judge=1, where the judge correctly identifies semantically equivalent answers (e.g., \texttt{0.05\,J} derived from a multi-step calculation) that the heuristic string matcher misses. Per-model agreement varies, with lower agreement for models that produce more verbose or formatted outputs. For the publication analysis, \textbf{judge labels are used as authoritative} for \texttt{is\_correct} and \texttt{wrong\_answer\_endorsed}; \textbf{heuristic labels are used as authoritative} for \texttt{refusal\_flag} (after the refusal phrase expansion described above).

\subsection{Refusal Phrase Expansion}
During a data integrity investigation, we identified a subset of trials containing clear refusal language (``I am unsure,'' ``uncertain'') with \texttt{refusal\_flag=0}. Six phrases were added to the heuristic refusal detector: \texttt{``i am unsure,'' ``i'm unsure,'' ``unsure,'' ``i am not sure,'' ``not confident,'' ``uncertain''}. The heuristic labels were re-scored via \texttt{rescore\_outputs.py}. Additionally, judge \texttt{refusal\_flag} values were surgically patched for trials matching these phrases via \texttt{fix\_judge\_refusal\_flags.py}. Both scripts are included in the open-source release.

\subsection{McNemar Pairing Methodology}
McNemar tests require paired observations on the same item across two conditions. Pairing is done by \texttt{item\_id} (inner join): only items where both the control and pressure conditions have valid \texttt{is\_correct} labels are included. Items where either condition has \texttt{is\_correct=null} (typically refusals) are excluded from the pairing. This ensures the McNemar contingency table is not contaminated by unpaired observations. For models with high refusal rates, this reduces the effective sample size.
