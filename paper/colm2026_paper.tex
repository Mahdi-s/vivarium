\begin{abstract}
We study social conformity in large language models---capitulation to fabricated peer consensus, distinct from user-directed sycophancy---across 144,008 trials spanning 10 model families. By evaluating four sequential OLMo-3-7B checkpoints that share identical pretraining, we observe that conformity to structured peer-consensus prompts increases following supervised fine-tuning (McNemar OR\,=\,15.5 vs.\ 8.8 for base, $p<0.001$) and partially decreases after direct preference optimization (OR\,=\,8.7). Extending to 10 diverse model families tested on 400 shared items under identical Asch-style pressure, we find that conformity susceptibility varies over 40-fold (OR\,=\,44 for Llama-3.1-70B to OR\,$\approx$\,1 for GPT-OSS-20B) and that models exhibit three distinct behavioral responses: endorsement of the wrong answer, refusal to answer, or insensitivity to the consensus context. In our sample, peer consensus induced significant conformity in a larger proportion of families (7/10) than authority framing (2/10). Models with chain-of-thought reasoning largely resist conformity on these prompts. A calibration bridge on identical items confirms that OLMo's conformity level is typical of instruct-tuned models. We note important caveats: the structured prompt format conflates social pressure with autoregressive pattern completion (\S\ref{sec:sft_dpo}), one evaluated model (GPT-OSS-20B) also serves as the judge for Study~2 (\S\ref{sec:discussion}), and error rates exclude refusals from the denominator, which inflates odds ratios for high-refusal models (Appendix~\ref{app:refusal}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The rapid deployment of large language models (LLMs) in decision-critical workflows demands rigorous investigation of their reliability under social pressure. A persistent failure mode is the tendency to prioritize agreement with contextual cues over the model's own internal knowledge, a behavior that can be catastrophic when LLMs serve as medical assistants, legal advisors, or coding copilots.

We study \textbf{social conformity}: capitulation to simulated \emph{peer pressure} embedded in the prompt context, mechanistically distinct from \textbf{sycophancy} (deference to the \emph{user's} opinion;~\citealp{sharma2024sycophancy}). Following the classical paradigm of \citet{asch1956}, we subject models to fabricated unanimous peer consensus and measure whether they abandon correct answers to agree with the majority.

\paragraph{Two complementary studies.}
Prior cross-family comparisons~\citep{zhu2025conformity} found instruction-tuned models \emph{less} conformist than base models, but could not determine whether this reflects alignment effects or architectural differences. We address this through two interlocking experiments. \textbf{Study~1} traces four sequential OLMo-3-7B checkpoints sharing identical pretraining across 115,200~trials, isolating how each post-training stage correlates with conformity changes: SFT is associated with \emph{increased} conformity while DPO is associated with a partial \emph{decrease}. \textbf{Study~2} evaluates 10~diverse model families on 400~shared items under identical Asch-style conditions (28,808~trials), revealing that conformity susceptibility varies over 40-fold (OR\,=\,44 to OR\,$\approx$\,1) and that models respond to pressure through qualitatively different behavioral patterns. A calibration bridge on identical items connects the two studies. An important caveat is that the structured prompt format used for peer consensus conflates social pressure with autoregressive pattern completion (\S\ref{sec:sft_dpo}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper provides five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Within-family decomposition}: SFT and DPO are associated with \emph{opposite} changes in conformity within the OLMo-3-7B pipeline. Because DPO is applied to SFT weights, these effects are not independently identifiable; we report the sequential trajectory (\S\ref{sec:sft_dpo}).
    \item \textbf{Cross-family behavioral patterns}: Under identical pressure, models exhibit three distinct response modes---endorsement of the wrong answer, refusal to answer, or insensitivity to the consensus context (\S\ref{sec:cross_family}).
    \item \textbf{Reasoning models show reduced conformity}: Three of four reasoning-capable models show non-significant McNemar tests on these prompts; the fourth (OLMo-7B-Think, OR\,=\,2.9) shows a small effect below its instruct sibling (\S\ref{sec:cross_family}).
    \item \textbf{Peer $>$ authority in our sample}: Peer consensus significantly increases errors for 7 of 10 families; authority framing for only 2 (Holm--Bonferroni corrected; \S\ref{sec:cross_family}).
    \item \textbf{Large cross-family variation}: Susceptibility spans OR\,=\,44.3 to OR\,$\approx$\,1.0 across families on identical items, though the comparison is complicated by differential refusal rates and the use of one evaluated model as the judge (\S\ref{sec:cross_family}, \S\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sycophancy vs.\ Social Conformity.}
Sycophancy studies \emph{user-directed} deference. \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} showed that human evaluators and preference models sometimes favor sycophantic responses; \citet{elephant2025} found that on personal advice queries, LLM action endorsement was 47\% higher than human responses; \citet{hong2025syconbench} showed alignment tuning amplifies the effect over multi-turn dialogue. \emph{Social conformity}, by contrast, studies \emph{peer-directed} deference to fabricated third-party consensus. The mechanisms differ: sycophancy exploits helpfulness training, while conformity exploits sensitivity to contextual consensus. A model resisting user sycophancy may still conform to peer pressure; our study targets the latter.

\paragraph{LLM Conformity.}
\citet{zhu2025conformity} is the most directly comparable work. They adapt Asch-style paradigms across four model families (Llama-3, Gemma, Mistral, Qwen2), test confederate unanimity and tone variation, and propose devil's advocate and question distillation as mitigations. Their central finding, that instruction-tuned models are less conformist than base models, is consistent with our Asch-5 (free-text) results but not with our structured-prompt results, where SFT amplifies conformity within the OLMo-3 pipeline. The discrepancy likely reflects differences in prompt format: our structured prompts assign the model an explicit participant role and establish an n-gram repetition pattern, which may interact with instruction-following training to amplify conformity (Appendix~\ref{app:format}). Our within-family design (four OLMo-3-7B checkpoints with identical pretraining) additionally reveals that SFT and DPO have opposing effects on conformity, a finding invisible in any end-to-end base-vs.-instruct comparison.

\citet{celebi2025parrot} evaluate 22 models on sycophancy under authority pressure across 13 MMLU domains with log-likelihood calibration, but do not decompose the post-training pipeline into stages. \citet{weng2025benchform} study conformity in multi-agent settings. \citet{shoval2025psychiatric} adapt Asch-style influence to psychiatric assessments using GPT-4o, but analyze a single model with no training-stage decomposition.

\paragraph{Post-Training Effects on Behavior.}
The RLHF pipeline reduces generation diversity and sharpens output distributions~\citep{rlhf_entropy}, potentially increasing the likelihood of sampling socially ``safe'' agreement modes. \citet{rafailov2023dpo} introduced DPO as an offline alternative to PPO~\citep{schulman2017ppo}. \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} showed that RLHF broadly amplifies sycophancy but did not isolate SFT from preference optimization as separate stages. Our design fills this gap: we show that SFT and DPO pull conformity in opposite directions, a finding that would be invisible in any study treating post-training as a single step.

\paragraph{Temperature and Conformity.}
\citet{renze2024} find temperature changes (0.0--1.0) do not significantly impact problem-solving in neutral contexts. Our data shows this finding does not generalize to biased contexts: conformity effects are robust across the full temperature range, with the majority of vulnerability concentrated at $T\!=\!0.0$ (greedy decoding). The $T_c$ metric we introduce provides the first per-item characterization of this temperature--conformity interaction.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Study 1: Within-Family Causal Design (OLMo-7B)}
\label{sec:study1_method}

We evaluate four OLMo-3-7B checkpoints along the post-training pipeline, sharing identical pretraining:
\textbf{Stage 0, Base} (pre-trained, no alignment);
\textbf{Stage 1, SFT} (supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations);
\textbf{Stage 2, DPO} (direct preference optimization);
\textbf{Stage 3, Instruct} (final checkpoint after RLVR).
Because all four share the same architecture and pretraining data, behavioral differences can be attributed to changes introduced during post-training, though the sequential application of stages means individual contributions are not independently identifiable. Each variant is evaluated on 12 Asch-style conditions (8~peer consensus, 3~authority, 1~control; Appendix~\ref{app:conditions}) at 6~temperatures ($T \in \{0.0, 0.2, \ldots, 1.0\}$), yielding 115,200~trials.

\subsection{Study 2: Cross-Family Observational Survey}
\label{sec:study2_method}

To test whether the SFT amplification finding generalizes beyond OLMo, we evaluate 10~model families spanning diverse architectures and alignment approaches: Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct~\citep{meta2024llama3} (dense, SFT+RLHF), Llama-4-Maverick~\citep{meta2025llama4} (MoE, lightweight SFT + online RL), GPT-4o-Mini~\citep{openai2024gpt4omini} (dense, RLHF + Instruction Hierarchy), GPT-OSS-20B~\citep{openai2025gptoss} (MoE, RL with unsupervised CoT), Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite~\citep{google2025gemini25} (MoE, distillation), Grok-4.1-Fast~\citep{xai2025grok41} (RL-dominant, tool-focused), OLMo-3.1-32B-Instruct and OLMo-3.1-32B-Think (dense), and OLMo-3-7B-Think. Each model is tested on 4~core conditions (control, structured peer consensus, authoritative bias, authority trust) at $T=0.0$ and $T=0.6$, with 50~items per dataset across 8~domains (400~items per condition). The same items are used across all models.

\subsection{Bridge Design}
\label{sec:bridge}

OLMo-7B-Instruct appears in both studies, tested on the same 400~items under the same 4~conditions. This calibration bridge validates that Study~1's within-family findings are not idiosyncratic by placing them in the cross-family ranking.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Framework}

Correctness is determined by a post-hoc LLM judge (GPT-OSS-20B for Study~2; multi-model ensemble for Study~1). The judge produces three labels per trial: \texttt{is\_correct} (factual accuracy), \texttt{refusal\_flag} (model refused to answer), and \texttt{wrong\_answer\_endorsed} (sycophantic capitulation). Pressure effect is defined as $\Delta = \text{Error}_p - \text{Error}_c$ where error excludes refusals from the denominator. McNemar's test with Yates correction (Holm--Bonferroni corrected) is used for paired pressure-vs.-control comparisons, with items matched by \texttt{item\_id}. Refusal rates are reported alongside error rates so readers can assess the impact of refusal exclusion (Appendix~\ref{app:refusal}).

The training trajectory reveals opposing forces (Figure~\ref{fig:or}). Following the true pipeline chronology (Base $\to$ SFT $\to$ DPO $\to$ Instruct), control error rates decrease from 0.580 to 0.500--0.523, but conformity susceptibility diverges dramatically: SFT amplifies the McNemar OR from 8.79 to 15.50, DPO reverses this to 8.69 (at the base level), and the final RLVR stage produces a partial relapse to 12.24.

\paragraph{The SFT Spike (Stage 1).}
SFT is associated with the largest conformity increase: OR jumps from 8.79 (Base) to 15.50, with the highest wrong-answer endorsement rate of any variant (39.2\%) and the \emph{lowest} refusal rate (19.3\%). SFT simultaneously suppresses the model's tendency to refuse and amplifies its tendency to comply. We hypothesize that SFT on human demonstrations may introduce a deference bias: if annotators exhibit patterns of conversational accommodation~\citep{giles1991cac}, the model may internalize and overgeneralize these patterns under social pressure. This hypothesis is consistent with \citet{sharma2024sycophancy}'s finding that human preference data favors sycophantic responses, but we have not empirically audited OLMo's SFT dataset to confirm this mechanism.

\paragraph{DPO as Partial Correction (Stage 2).}
DPO reverses the SFT spike, reducing OR from 15.50 to 8.69 and endorsement from 39.2\% to 20.7\%, while restoring a higher refusal rate (42.8\%). DPO training, which optimizes for preferred over dispreferred outputs, appears to counteract the sycophantic prior. The final RLVR stage (Instruct) produces a partial relapse (OR\,=\,12.24), though all variants remain significantly vulnerable ($p < 0.001$).


\paragraph{Format confound and mitigations.} An important caveat: under the classical free-text Asch-5 format, Instruct and DPO show \textbf{no significant conformity} ($\Delta=-0.040$, ns). Conformity emerges only under the structured participant-role format ($\Delta=+0.219$), which enforces a 5-shot n-gram repetition pattern (``Participant 1: X $\ldots$ Participant 5: X''). What we measure may therefore partly reflect autoregressive pattern completion rather than sensitivity to social consensus per se. We did not run a non-social pattern-completion baseline, which limits the construct validity of our ``conformity'' label. Devil's advocate mitigations~\citep{zhu2025conformity} reduce but do not eliminate the effect (Appendix~\ref{app:format},~\ref{app:mitigation}).

\paragraph{Temperature analysis.} We introduce \emph{Conformity Temperature} ($T_c$), the lowest temperature at which each item first conforms. A narrow majority of conforming items (53--54\%) yield at $T\!=\!0$ (greedy decoding), rising to 61.8\% for SFT and dropping to 46.1\% for DPO. This suggests the conforming response is the model's highest-probability output for these items, though 46--47\% require stochastic sampling to manifest (Appendix~\ref{app:tc}).

\section{Cross-Family Conformity Landscape}
\label{sec:cross_family}

Study~2 evaluates 10 model families on 400~shared items under the same structured peer-consensus condition used in Study~1 (5~unanimous confederates, confident tone). All models are tested at $T=0.0$ and $T=0.6$ with identical prompts. This section reports five main findings.

\subsection{The Conformity Spectrum}

Figure~\ref{fig:forest} ranks all models by their mean peer-pressure effect ($\Delta_\text{peer}$), including the four OLMo-7B training stages from the calibration bridge. Conformity susceptibility spans two orders of magnitude in McNemar odds ratio: from OR\,=\,44.3 (Llama-3.1-70B at $T=0.0$, where 133 items flip from correct to incorrect under pressure while only 3 flip in the other direction) to OR\,$\approx$\,1.0 (GPT-OSS-20B, where pressure is equally likely to help as to hurt). Seven of ten families show statistically significant peer conformity ($p_\text{Holm} < 0.05$); three do not (OLMo-32B-Think, Grok-4.1-Fast, GPT-OSS-20B).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3_cross_family_forest.pdf}
    \caption{Cross-family conformity ranking under structured peer consensus (Asch-style, 5 unanimous confident confederates). Models ranked by mean $\Delta_\text{peer}$ across $T=0.0$ and $T=0.6$. Right columns show McNemar OR and Holm-corrected significance ($^{***}p<0.001$, $^{**}p<0.01$, $^{*}p<0.05$, ns = non-significant). Grey rows: OLMo-7B training stages from calibration bridge. All models share the same 400 evaluation items.}
    \label{fig:forest}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Behavioral Taxonomy: Endorse, Refuse, or Resist}

Models respond to identical social pressure through three qualitatively distinct behavioral patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endorsement-dominant.} GPT-4o-Mini endorses the wrong answer on 42\% of trials while refusing on only 4\%. OLMo-32B-Instruct shows a similar profile (45\% endorsement, 31\% refusal).
    \item \textbf{Refusal-dominant.} Llama-3.1-70B triggers a refusal response on 84\% of peer-pressure trials (avg.\ across $T=0.0$ and $T=0.6$), endorsing the wrong answer on only 5\%. This high refusal rate means that the model's McNemar OR (44.3 at $T=0.0$) is computed on a reduced subset of items where it actually answered in both conditions ($n=259$ of 400; see Appendix~\ref{app:refusal} for the impact of this denominator choice).
    \item \textbf{Context-insensitive.} Grok-4.1-Fast (8\% endorsement, 5\% refusal, OR\,=\,1.1) and GPT-OSS-20B (19\% endorsement, 3\% refusal, OR\,=\,1.0) neither endorse nor refuse at elevated rates. \emph{Important caveat:} GPT-OSS-20B also serves as the judge for Study~2 (\S\ref{sec:discussion}), so its apparent insensitivity may partly reflect self-preference bias in the judging process.
\end{itemize}

These patterns are \emph{not} a simple function of overall capability: Grok-4.1-Fast has the lowest control error (0.317) while Llama-3.1-70B has a similar control error (0.352), yet they respond to pressure in opposite ways. The behavioral pattern may correlate with the post-training approach, though we cannot establish this for closed-source models whose training details are not fully disclosed.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{fig4_refusal_endorsement.pdf}
    \caption{Behavioral response taxonomy under peer consensus pressure. Each model plotted by endorsement rate (x-axis) vs.\ refusal rate (y-axis) at $T=0.0$. Point size proportional to $|\Delta_\text{peer}|$. McNemar OR and significance annotated per model. Dashed lines delineate three behavioral quadrants: endorsement-dominant (high endorsement), refusal-dominant (high refusal), and context-insensitive (low both).}
    \label{fig:scatter}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Think Models Resist Peer Conformity}

Three of four models with chain-of-thought reasoning capability show non-significant peer conformity after Holm--Bonferroni correction: OLMo-32B-Think (OR\,=\,1.45, $p_\text{Holm}=1.0$), Grok-4.1-Fast (OR\,=\,1.16, $p_\text{Holm}=1.0$), and GPT-OSS-20B (OR\,=\,0.98, $p_\text{Holm}=1.0$). OLMo-7B-Think shows a small but significant effect (OR\,=\,2.88, $p_\text{Holm}=0.01$), substantially lower than its non-think sibling OLMo-7B-Instruct ($\Delta=+0.430$). In contrast, instruct variants of the same families are highly vulnerable: OLMo-32B-Instruct (OR\,=\,19.0, $p<0.001$). Extended reasoning---generating intermediate logical steps before committing to a final answer---appears to provide substantial defense against social consensus, though the smallest think model (7B) retains marginal vulnerability.

\subsection{Peer Consensus $\gg$ Authority Framing}

Figure~\ref{fig:peer_auth} compares pressure effects across two mechanisms. Peer consensus ($\Delta_\text{peer}$) is statistically significant for 7 of 10 families after Holm--Bonferroni correction. Authority framing ($\Delta_\text{auth}$) is significant for only 2 of 10 families (Llama-3-8B and Llama-3.1-70B, both dense models with heavy SFT). The structured peer consensus prompt, which establishes an explicit n-gram repetition pattern across 5~fabricated participants, exploits autoregressive pattern-completion in a way that authority framing (a single user claim) cannot.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_peer_vs_authority.pdf}
    \caption{Peer consensus vs.\ authority framing across model families. Models sorted by $\Delta_\text{peer}$. Significance annotations from McNemar tests (Holm corrected). Peer consensus is significant for 7/10 families; authority framing for 2/10.}
    \label{fig:peer_auth}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Bridge Validation: OLMo Is Not Idiosyncratic}

The OLMo-7B-Instruct variant, which appears in both studies on identical items, ranks 3rd in the cross-family conformity ranking ($\Delta_\text{peer}=+0.430$), between Llama-3.1-70B ($+0.457$) and Llama-3-8B ($+0.374$). The within-family SFT amplification is visible in the ranking: OLMo-SFT ($+0.416$) sits above OLMo-Base ($+0.341$), and OLMo-DPO ($+0.399$) sits below OLMo-SFT, consistent with the within-family trajectory in Study~1. This placement confirms that OLMo's conformity level is typical of instruct-tuned models and that the SFT-associated conformity increase observed in Study~1 is not idiosyncratic to OLMo.


%% ============================================================
\section{Architectural and Alignment Correlates}
\label{sec:arch}

The cross-family data reveals suggestive patterns that warrant discussion, though the observational design prevents attribution to any single factor.

\paragraph{MoE models show lower conformity in our sample.} All three MoE models (Llama-4-Maverick, Gemini-Flash-Lite, GPT-OSS-20B) show $\Delta_\text{peer} < +0.14$, while all dense non-think instruct models show $\Delta > +0.24$. With only 3 MoE and 5 dense models, this sample is too small for meaningful statistical testing, and the pattern is fully confounded with alignment strategy, model size, and training data. We report it as a descriptive observation.

\paragraph{Models with reasoning capability show lower conformity.} The four think/reasoning models ($\Delta$ mean $= +0.04$) all fall below the six non-think models ($\Delta$ mean $= +0.30$). With only four reasoning models and no formal group test, this is a descriptive pattern consistent with Study~1's OLMo-Think results.

\paragraph{Alignment strategy correlates.} Across families, models whose published documentation describes heavy SFT~\citep{meta2024llama3} show higher conformity than those with lighter SFT~\citep{meta2025llama4} or RL-dominant approaches~\citep{openai2025gptoss, xai2025grok41}. This gradient is consistent with Study~1's within-family observation, but the cross-family evidence is observational. We cannot attribute differences to alignment strategy alone because architecture, model size, training data, and undisclosed engineering choices all differ simultaneously. Furthermore, the training recipes of closed-source models are only partially disclosed.


%% ============================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{SFT correlates with increased conformity.}
Study~1 observed that conformity increased following SFT and partially decreased following DPO within OLMo. Because DPO is applied to SFT weights, the DPO effect is confounded by SFT initialization and cannot be interpreted as an independent contribution. Furthermore, the observed changes may reflect OLMo's specific training datasets rather than universal properties of SFT or DPO algorithms. Study~2 provides cross-family corroboration: models whose published documentation describes heavy SFT cluster at the top of the conformity ranking, while those with RL-dominant alignment sit at the bottom. The bridge confirms OLMo-Instruct is typical, not idiosyncratic.

\paragraph{Different post-training approaches produce distinct behavioral responses.}
\citet{zhu2025conformity}'s finding that instruct models are less conformist is not wrong---it is incomplete. Our data shows that different post-training pipelines produce distinct behavioral responses to identical prompts. Llama-3.1-70B primarily refuses (84\% refusal rate); GPT-4o-Mini primarily endorses (42\% endorsement). We cannot determine from behavioral data alone whether these patterns ``reflect'' the dominant training objective, as the training objectives and reward functions of closed-source models are not publicly disclosed. The correlation between published alignment approach and behavioral pattern is suggestive but not definitive.

\paragraph{Reasoning models and conformity.}
Three of four reasoning-capable models show non-significant peer conformity, and the fourth shows a reduced effect. This is consistent with the hypothesis that generating intermediate logical steps may anchor the model before encountering social pressure, though we cannot rule out other explanations (e.g., reasoning models may simply be less sensitive to in-context patterns generally). We did not test whether a non-social pattern-completion task would produce the same result.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\textbf{Self-judge bias}: GPT-OSS-20B serves as the sole judge for Study~2 while also being evaluated as one of the 10 target models. Its apparent insensitivity to social pressure (OR\,$\approx$\,1) may partly reflect self-preference bias---a documented phenomenon in LLM-as-judge evaluations. This is the most serious threat to our cross-family ranking. Future work should use an independent judge or human annotation for this model. \textbf{Denominator exclusion}: error rates exclude refusals, inflating ORs for high-refusal models (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B's OR=44.3 is computed on 259/400 paired items). Appendix~\ref{app:refusal} shows the qualitative ranking is preserved under alternative denominators, but quantitative ORs differ substantially. \textbf{Construct validity}: the structured prompt conflates social pressure with n-gram pattern completion; under the classical Asch format, post-training models show no significant conformity. Without a non-social pattern-completion baseline, we cannot determine how much of the measured effect is ``social conformity'' versus autoregressive completion. \textbf{Sequential design}: Study~1 traces a single pipeline; observed changes may reflect OLMo's specific datasets. \textbf{Scope}: we evaluated 10 model families but excluded Anthropic's Claude models, which pioneered Constitutional AI for sycophancy mitigation. Study~2 uses 4~conditions and 2~temperatures. \textbf{Missing baselines}: we did not test whether a simple system prompt (e.g., ``maintain strict factual accuracy'') eliminates the effect, which would distinguish deep alignment failure from trivial instruction-following artifacts.


%% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Within the OLMo-7B pipeline, conformity to structured peer-consensus prompts increases following SFT and partially decreases following DPO, though these effects are confounded by sequential application and OLMo-specific training data. Across 10~model families tested on identical items, conformity susceptibility varies over 40-fold (McNemar OR\,=\,44 to OR\,$\approx$\,1), and models exhibit three distinct behavioral responses: endorsement, refusal, or insensitivity to the consensus context. In our sample, peer consensus induced significant conformity in more families (7/10) than authority framing (2/10), and reasoning-capable models showed reduced susceptibility. These findings are subject to important caveats: the structured prompt format conflates social pressure with autoregressive pattern completion, one evaluated model serves as its own judge, and error rates exclude refusals from the denominator. Despite these limitations, the results suggest that vulnerability to in-context consensus is not a generic LLM property but varies substantially across models and may correlate with post-training approach. We release the full evaluation pipeline and all data as open-source code.

\section{LLM Judge Protocol}
\label{app:judge}

All reported behavioral metrics use a post-hoc multi-model LLM judge ensemble (Qwen3-1B/8B, Gemma-3-1B, GPT-oss-20B) and require valid parsed judge JSON. The judge receives the question, ground truth, injected wrong answer, and model completion, and must return a structured decision.

\begin{promptbox}
[Judge Prompt Template]

You are an evaluation judge for LLM conformity experiments.
Given:
- Question: <question>
- Ground truth answer: <ground_truth_text>
- Injected wrong answer: <wrong_answer>
- Model response: <model_output>

Return ONLY valid JSON with this schema:
{
  "is_correct": 0 or 1,
  "wrong_answer_endorsed": 0 or 1,
  "refusal_flag": 0 or 1,
  "notes": "<short rationale>"
}

Labeling rules:
1) is_correct=1 only if the response supports the ground truth answer.
2) wrong_answer_endorsed=1 only if the response clearly endorses the injected wrong answer.
3) refusal_flag=1 for abstentions/refusals/non-answers.
4) Output JSON only, no extra text.
\end{promptbox}

The judge output schema contains four fields: \texttt{is\_correct}, \texttt{wrong\_answer\_endorsed}, \texttt{refusal\_flag}, and \texttt{notes}. Trials where the judge JSON is missing or fails to parse are excluded from all judge-based analyses. All primary metrics---error rate, truth override, truth rescue, and wrong-answer flip---are derived exclusively from these judge fields. Raw model completions and parsed judge outputs are retained per trial in the experiment database to support reproduction and auditing.

\section{Cell Balance Verification}
\label{app:balance}

Our experimental design targets equal trial counts across all (variant, condition, temperature) cells. We verified balance by computing the deviation of each cell's trial count from the per-temperature median. Across 264 non-control pressure cells, the maximum deviation is 10.5\% (authoritative bias at $T\!=\!0.2$ for instruct), and the mean absolute deviation is 1.0\%. The 18 cells exceeding the 5\% threshold are concentrated in two conditions (authoritative bias and Asch history) at low temperatures ($T \leq 0.6$), reflecting minor item-level filtering differences that do not affect the pooled statistical tests. Control cells have lower counts by design, as they are matched with each pressure condition separately.

\section{Format Effect Analysis}
\label{app:format}

Under the classical free-text Asch-5 format, Instruct and DPO show no significant conformity ($\Delta=-0.040$, ns). Structured participant-role framing produces $\Delta=+0.219$ for the same content---a $+$22pp effect from format alone. This is consistent with the autoregressive pattern-completion hypothesis: the structured prompt assigns the model a participant role and establishes an n-gram repetition pattern across 5 fabricated peers, which SFT-trained models are compelled to complete. Full format comparison data and the Asch-5 vs.\ structured prompt examples are available in the supplementary materials.

\section{Conformity Temperature and URSP}
\label{app:tc}

We define \emph{Conformity Temperature} ($T_c$) as the lowest temperature at which each (item, variant, condition) triple conforms. For both Base and Instruct, 53--54\% of conforming items yield at $T\!=\!0$ (greedy decoding). SFT pushes this to 61.8\%; DPO pulls it to 46.1\%. Among conforming trials, 18--24\% exhibit ground-truth leakage (URSP)---the output contains the correct answer despite endorsing the wrong one---though this rate is confounded by output verbosity (median trace length: SFT 66 chars vs.\ Base 509 chars).

\section{Mitigation Effectiveness}
\label{app:mitigation}

Devil's advocate (DA) significantly reduces conformity for all variants: SFT agreement rate drops by 5.5pp ($p < 10^{-12}$), Base by 3.9pp ($p < 10^{-4}$), and Instruct by 3.7pp ($p < 10^{-6}$). Question distillation (QD) is less effective and shows no significant effect for Instruct ($p \approx 1.0$). No prompt-level mitigation eliminates conformity for any variant.

\section{Refusal Handling}
\label{app:refusal}

\paragraph{Two separate fields.} \texttt{refusal\_flag} and \texttt{is\_correct} are independent judge outputs. A trial can have \texttt{refusal\_flag=1} (the response contains refusal language) \emph{and} \texttt{is\_correct=0} (the judge could still determine the answer was factually wrong). For Llama-3.1-70B at $T=0.0$: 356/400 peer-pressure trials have \texttt{refusal\_flag=1}, but 226 of those 356 also have a valid \texttt{is\_correct} score. Only 130/400 have \texttt{is\_correct=null}, yielding 270 valid items for error rate computation and 259 paired items for McNemar (after intersection with control). Thus the 89\% refusal rate and the $n=259$ McNemar sample are \emph{not} contradictory.

\paragraph{Denominator choice and selection bias.} When the judge sets \texttt{is\_correct=null}, that trial is excluded from the error rate denominator. Error rates thus reflect ``accuracy among trials where the judge could determine correctness,'' not ``accuracy among all trials.'' This creates a \textbf{selection bias}: for high-refusal models, the McNemar test operates on a non-representative subset of items where the safety filter did not fully suppress the response. This inflates Odds Ratios for evasive models (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B's OR=44.3 is computed on 259 items, not 400) while models with near-complete response rates (GPT-4o-Mini: 380/400 paired) face no such inflation.

We report three alternative denominator treatments for Llama-3.1-70B at $T=0.0$: (a) excluding nulls (current; $\Delta=+0.533$), (b) counting refusals as errors ($\Delta=+0.542$), (c) counting refusals as correct ($\Delta=+0.260$). The qualitative ranking is preserved under (a) and (b); under (c) the effect is substantially attenuated. Refusal rates are reported alongside all error rates so readers can assess the impact of this choice.


\section{Judge Protocol Details}
\label{app:judge_details}

\subsection{Judge Model Selection and Self-Evaluation Caveat}
Study~1 (OLMo family) uses a multi-model judge ensemble: Gemma-3-1B, Gemma-3-12B, Qwen3-0.6B, and GPT-OSS-20B, applied incrementally as each judge became available. Study~2 (cross-family) uses a single judge model: GPT-OSS-20B~\citep{openai2025gptoss} via OpenRouter, with \texttt{temperature=0.0} and \texttt{reasoning.effort=low}.

\textbf{Self-evaluation conflict:} GPT-OSS-20B is simultaneously one of the 10 evaluated target models \emph{and} the sole judge for Study~2. Self-preference bias in LLM-as-judge settings is a documented phenomenon~\citep{zheng2023judging}. GPT-OSS-20B emerges as the most conformity-resistant model in our ranking (OR\,$\approx$\,1.0). We cannot rule out that this result is inflated by self-preference. This is the most serious validity threat to the cross-family ranking. Future work should validate GPT-OSS-20B's results using an independent judge or human annotation.

\subsection{Heuristic Scoring Pipeline}
Before judge labels are applied, each trial undergoes a four-phase heuristic extraction cascade:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase A (answer extraction):} Strip \texttt{</think>} tags, extract final answer span via explicit markers (\texttt{Answer:}, \texttt{$\backslash$boxed\{\}}), or tail-window heuristic.
    \item \textbf{Phase B (correctness matching):} Compare extracted answer against ground truth using substring matching, numeric comparison, and negation-aware context windows.
    \item \textbf{Phase C (wrong-answer detection):} Check for endorsement of the injected wrong answer with safeguards against negation, reported speech, and prompt echo.
    \item \textbf{Phase D (refusal detection):} Scan post-think content for refusal phrases (23 patterns including ``I am unsure,'' ``uncertain,'' ``I don't know,'' added during the data integrity audit).
\end{enumerate}
The heuristic labels populate the SQL columns (\texttt{is\_correct}, \texttt{refusal\_flag}) and serve as cross-validation for the judge labels.

\subsection{Heuristic vs.\ Judge Agreement}
Across all 144,008 in-scope trials, the heuristic and judge labels agree on \texttt{is\_correct} for 77.4\% of comparable trials and on \texttt{refusal\_flag} for 88.4\%. Disagreement is overwhelmingly one-directional: 74.5\% of \texttt{is\_correct} mismatches are heur=0/judge=1, where the judge correctly identifies semantically equivalent answers (e.g., \texttt{0.05$\backslash$\,text\{J\}} vs.\ \texttt{0.05 J}) that the heuristic string matcher misses. Per-model agreement ranges from 54.7\% (OLMo-32B-Think) to 78.4\% (OLMo-7B-Base), with lower agreement for models that produce more verbose or formatted outputs. For the publication analysis, \textbf{judge labels are used as authoritative} for \texttt{is\_correct} and \texttt{wrong\_answer\_endorsed}; \textbf{heuristic labels are used as authoritative} for \texttt{refusal\_flag} (after the refusal phrase expansion described above).

\subsection{Refusal Phrase Expansion}
During the data integrity investigation, we identified 20,428 trials (8.4\%) containing clear refusal language (``I am unsure,'' ``uncertain'') with \texttt{refusal\_flag=0}. Six phrases were added to the heuristic refusal detector: \texttt{``i am unsure,'' ``i'm unsure,'' ``unsure,'' ``i am not sure,'' ``not confident,'' ``uncertain''}. The heuristic labels were re-scored via \texttt{rescore\_outputs.py}. Additionally, 25,320 judge \texttt{refusal\_flag} values were surgically patched to 1 for trials matching these phrases via \texttt{fix\_judge\_refusal\_flags.py}. Both scripts are included in the open-source release.

\subsection{McNemar Pairing Methodology}
McNemar tests require paired observations on the same item across two conditions. Pairing is done by \texttt{item\_id} (inner join): only items where both the control and pressure conditions have valid \texttt{is\_correct} labels are included. Items where either condition has \texttt{is\_correct=null} (typically refusals) are excluded from the pairing. This ensures the McNemar contingency table is not contaminated by unpaired observations. For models with high refusal rates, this reduces the effective sample size (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B: 259/400 items paired at $T=0.0$).
