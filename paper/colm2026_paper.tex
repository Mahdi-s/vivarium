\begin{abstract}
Large language models are increasingly deployed in roles where they must maintain factual accuracy under contextual pressure. We study \emph{consensus susceptibility}---the tendency of a model to abandon a correct answer when fabricated peer consensus in the prompt endorses a wrong one---through two complementary studies. First, we decompose the post-training pipeline of a single open-weight model family (OLMo-3-7B) into its constituent stages---supervised fine-tuning (SFT), direct preference optimization (DPO), and reinforcement learning---revealing that these stages have opposing effects on susceptibility: SFT amplifies wrong-answer endorsement while DPO partially reverses it. Second, we survey a diverse set of model families on identical items under identical pressure, uncovering three qualitatively distinct behavioral modes---endorsement-dominant, refusal-dominant, and context-insensitive---shaped by alignment approach rather than raw capability. Two ablation studies probe the mechanism underlying the effect and reveal that a substantial portion arises from autoregressive pattern completion rather than genuine social deference: stripping all social framing from the prompt while preserving its repetitive structure still elicits high endorsement. Together, these findings reframe consensus susceptibility as primarily a vulnerability to repetitive in-context patterns whose severity is governed by post-training decisions, with direct implications for alignment pipeline design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

As large language models (LLMs) are deployed in decision-critical roles~\citep{bommasani2021opportunities}, their reliability depends not only on what they know but on whether they maintain that knowledge under contextual pressure. When an LLM serves as a medical assistant, legal advisor, or coding copilot, deference to misleading context can have serious consequences---yet our understanding of \emph{where} this vulnerability comes from remains surprisingly shallow.

We study \textbf{consensus susceptibility}: capitulation to fabricated \emph{peer consensus} embedded in the prompt. This is mechanistically distinct from \textbf{sycophancy} (deference to the \emph{user's} stated opinion;~\citealp{sharma2024sycophancy}). A sycophantic model agrees with the person asking the question; a consensus-susceptible model agrees with a crowd of fabricated peers, even when it would otherwise answer correctly. Following \citet{asch1956}'s classic conformity paradigm from social psychology, we present models with unanimous wrong answers from fabricated peers and measure whether they abandon correct responses to agree.

\paragraph{Why existing work leaves a gap.}
Prior cross-family comparisons~\citep{zhu2025conformity} tested base versus instruction-tuned models across several families and concluded that instruction-tuning \emph{reduces} conformity. But comparing a base Llama to an instruct Gemma tells us little about what drives the difference: is it the architecture, the pretraining data, the fine-tuning recipe, or all three at once? To answer the causal question---\emph{which stage of post-training introduces or removes this vulnerability?}---we need to compare checkpoints along the \emph{same} training pipeline, holding everything else constant. This has not been done for conformity.

\paragraph{Our approach: two complementary studies.}
We address this gap through two studies sharing a common evaluation protocol and item set.

\textbf{Study~1 (within-family decomposition)} exploits the OLMo-3-7B model family~\citep{groeneveld2024olmo}, which is unique among major model families in publicly releasing every intermediate training checkpoint. We evaluate four stages along the Instruct pipeline---the pretrained Base model, the SFT checkpoint, the DPO checkpoint, and the final released model---on the same items under the same conditions. Because all four share identical architecture and pretraining, any behavioral differences must reflect the post-training stage that produced them. This design reveals that successive stages have \emph{opposing} effects on susceptibility.

\textbf{Study~2 (cross-family survey)} tests whether the patterns observed within OLMo generalize. We evaluate a diverse set of model families---spanning dense and mixture-of-experts architectures, open and closed weights, and varied alignment approaches including RLHF, Constitutional AI, and RL-dominant training---on identical items under identical pressure. This survey uncovers qualitatively distinct behavioral modes: models do not simply vary in \emph{how much} they conform, but in \emph{how} they respond to pressure.

Two ablation studies then probe a critical confound in the experimental design: our Asch-style prompt embeds a repetitive n-gram pattern across the fabricated peer responses. Is the model conforming to \emph{social pressure}, or is it simply completing a \emph{syntactic pattern}? The ablations disentangle these two drivers (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Within-family decomposition}: We show that SFT and DPO are associated with \emph{opposite} changes in consensus susceptibility within the OLMo-3-7B pipeline, a finding invisible in any end-to-end base-vs.-instruct comparison (\S\ref{sec:sft_dpo}).
    \item \textbf{Cross-family behavioral taxonomy}: Under identical pressure, diverse model families exhibit three distinct response modes---endorsement-dominant, refusal-dominant, or context-insensitive---revealing that post-training approach, not just capability, shapes how models fail under pressure (\S\ref{sec:cross_family}).
    \item \textbf{Reasoning models}: All reasoning-capable models in our survey show non-significant conformity effects, a descriptive pattern we report without attributing it to any single shared property (\S\ref{sec:cross_family}).
    \item \textbf{Ablation reframes the mechanism}: Stripping social framing while preserving the repetitive prompt structure reveals that a substantial portion of the measured effect arises from autoregressive pattern completion rather than social deference (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sycophancy vs.\ Social Conformity.}
A growing body of work studies \emph{sycophancy}---models deferring to the user's stated opinion. \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} showed that human evaluators and preference models sometimes favor sycophantic responses, suggesting the problem is partly baked into the training signal. \citet{elephant2025} demonstrated that LLMs are substantially more likely than humans to endorse user actions on personal advice queries. \citet{hong2025syconbench} showed that alignment tuning amplifies sycophancy over multi-turn dialogue. These studies share a common framing: the pressure comes from \emph{the user asking the question}.

\emph{Social conformity} is a different phenomenon. Here, the pressure comes not from the user but from \emph{fabricated third-party peers} embedded in the prompt---a crowd of voices all endorsing the same wrong answer. The mechanisms differ: sycophancy exploits helpfulness training (``be agreeable to the person you're helping''), while conformity exploits sensitivity to contextual consensus (``everyone else said X''). A model that resists user sycophancy may still conform to peer pressure. Our study targets the latter, drawing on the social psychology tradition of \citet{asch1956} rather than the RLHF alignment literature.

\paragraph{LLM Conformity.}
\citet{zhu2025conformity} is the most directly comparable and influential work for our study. They adapt Asch-style paradigms across four model families (Llama-3, Gemma, Mistral, Qwen2), systematically vary confederate unanimity and tone, and propose devil's advocate and question distillation as mitigations. We adopt several elements of their experimental design---including the structured participant-role format, tone variations, and mitigation conditions---and extend them in three directions.

First, Zhu et al.'s central finding is that instruction-tuned models are \emph{less} conformist than their base counterparts. However, their comparison is between base and final instruct models, treating the entire post-training pipeline as a single step. Because base and instruct models differ in SFT data, preference optimization, and (sometimes) reinforcement learning simultaneously, this design cannot identify which stage is responsible for the observed difference. Our within-family design decomposes this pipeline into its constituent stages using a model family that publicly releases each intermediate checkpoint.

Second, Zhu et al.\ compare models across families, where architectural and data differences confound any alignment-related conclusions. Our Study~2 replicates this cross-family approach on a broader set of models, but anchors it to the within-family trajectory from Study~1, allowing us to distinguish family-level variation from alignment-stage effects.

Third, Zhu et al.\ do not probe the mechanism behind the measured effect. Our ablation studies (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) test whether the observed conformity reflects genuine social deference or autoregressive pattern completion driven by the repetitive structure of the Asch-style prompt---a distinction with direct implications for mitigation strategy.

Other recent work on LLM conformity includes \citet{celebi2025parrot}, who evaluate sycophancy under authority pressure across MMLU domains with log-likelihood calibration but do not decompose training stages; \citet{weng2025benchform}, who study conformity in multi-agent settings; and \citet{shoval2025psychiatric}, who adapt Asch-style influence to psychiatric assessments using a single model. None of these decompose the post-training pipeline.

\paragraph{Post-Training Effects on Behavior.}
Understanding how each stage of post-training shapes model behavior is a growing area of interest. The RLHF pipeline is known to reduce generation diversity and sharpen output distributions~\citep{rlhf_entropy}, potentially increasing the likelihood of sampling socially ``safe'' agreement modes. \citet{rafailov2023dpo} introduced DPO as an offline alternative to PPO~\citep{schulman2017ppo}, and it has become a standard component of modern alignment pipelines. \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} showed that RLHF broadly amplifies sycophancy but did not isolate SFT from preference optimization as separate stages---a gap our within-family design is specifically built to address. By evaluating checkpoints at each stage of the same pipeline, we can observe how conformity metrics change from one training stage to the next, even though the sequential application means we cannot fully isolate independent causal effects.

\paragraph{Temperature and Conformity.}
\citet{renze2024} find that temperature changes ($T \in [0.0, 1.0]$) do not significantly impact problem-solving accuracy in neutral (unbiased) contexts. An open question is whether this robustness holds when the context contains misleading pressure. Our experimental design tests the full temperature range, allowing us to characterize how decoding temperature interacts with conformity effects (Appendix~\ref{app:tc}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Study 1: Within-Family Sequential Design (OLMo-7B)}
\label{sec:study1_method}

We evaluate four OLMo-3-7B checkpoints along the Instruct post-training pipeline, all sharing identical architecture and pretraining data:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Base}: the pretrained model with no post-training.
    \item \textbf{Instruct\_SFT}: Base + supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on human demonstrations.
    \item \textbf{Instruct\_DPO}: Instruct\_SFT + direct preference optimization (DPO) on preference pairs.
    \item \textbf{Instruct}: the fully-trained released model (SFT + DPO + RLVR).
\end{itemize}
Because all four share the same pretrained weights, behavioral differences reflect changes introduced by each successive post-training stage, though the sequential application means individual contributions are not independently identifiable.

Each variant is evaluated on 12 conditions that vary the type and intensity of contextual pressure: 1 control condition (the question alone, with no pressure), 8 peer-consensus conditions (varying the unanimity, tone, and framing of fabricated peer responses), and 3 authority-claim conditions (a single user asserting the wrong answer with varying confidence). Each condition presents the same question with the same injected wrong answer; only the surrounding context changes (full catalog in Appendix~\ref{app:conditions}). Evaluation spans 6~temperatures ($T \in \{0.0, 0.2, \ldots, 1.0\}$). Because OLMo-7B-Instruct appears in both studies on the same 400 items and 4 conditions, it serves as a calibration reference linking the within-family trajectory to the cross-family ranking (\S\ref{sec:cross_family}).

\subsection{Study 2: Cross-Family Observational Survey}
\label{sec:study2_method}

To test whether the within-family findings generalize beyond OLMo, we evaluate a diverse set of model families spanning dense and mixture-of-experts architectures, open and closed weights, and varied alignment approaches including SFT+RLHF~\citep{meta2024llama3}, lightweight SFT + online RL~\citep{meta2025llama4}, RLHF with Instruction Hierarchy~\citep{openai2024gpt4omini}, RL with unsupervised chain-of-thought~\citep{openai2025gptoss}, distillation~\citep{google2025gemini25}, RL-dominant training~\citep{xai2025grok41}, and Constitutional AI~\citep{bai2022constitutional}. Each model is tested on 4~core conditions (control, structured peer consensus, authoritative bias, authority trust) at $T{=}0.0$ and $T{=}0.6$, with 50~items per dataset across 8~domains (400~items per condition, fixed $N{=}400$ denominator). The same items are used across all models. All inferential analyses in the main text use $T{=}0.0$ exclusively to preserve statistical independence across items; $T{=}0.6$ results are included in Appendix~\ref{app:cross_mcnemar} for completeness. Two additional ablation conditions (system-prompt removal, non-social n-gram baseline) are tested on selected models at $T{=}0.0$.

\subsection{Evaluation Pipeline}

Trial outputs were classified by two independent methods. First, a deterministic rule-based parser extracted each model's answer and classified it as correct, incorrect, or a refusal. Second, GPT-OSS-20B~\citep{openai2025gptoss} served as an LLM judge on the same outputs. For disagreements between the two methods, we used the judge's labels as authoritative for correctness (it better handles semantic equivalences, e.g., recognizing a derived numerical answer as correct) and the parser's labels as authoritative for refusal detection (after expanding the refusal phrase list based on manual review; see Appendix~\ref{app:judge_details}). Because GPT-OSS-20B is also one of the evaluated models, we note that the majority of its own labels were assigned by the rule-based parser, limiting self-preference bias to edge cases.

Every trial is assigned to one of three mutually exclusive states: \textbf{State~A} (correct answer), \textbf{State~B} (wrong-answer endorsement), or \textbf{State~C} (refusal). All proportions use a fixed denominator of $N{=}400$ items per condition. Refusals count toward the error rate but are tracked separately, since a model that refuses is failing differently from one that endorses a wrong answer. The pressure effect is $\Delta = \text{Error}_{\text{pressure}} - \text{Error}_{\text{control}}$. This 3-state decomposition is essential: a model with a large $\Delta$ driven entirely by increased refusals has a categorically different failure mode than one whose $\Delta$ comes from endorsing wrong answers. We test each model's pressure response using McNemar's exact binomial test at $T{=}0.0$, with Holm--Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (details on odds ratio computation and confidence intervals in Appendix~\ref{app:stat_methods}).

\paragraph{SFT-Associated Increase.}
Under structured peer consensus, the SFT checkpoint produces the highest wrong-answer endorsement of any variant---substantially exceeding even the unaligned Base model. SFT simultaneously shows the lowest refusal rate, suggesting that supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations suppresses the model's tendency to abstain while amplifying its tendency to reproduce patterns encountered in context. This is consistent with \citet{sharma2024sycophancy}'s finding that human preference data favors agreeable responses, though we have not audited OLMo's SFT dataset to confirm a specific mechanism.

\paragraph{DPO-Associated Decrease.}
DPO is associated with reduced susceptibility: endorsement drops and refusal rises relative to SFT. The final RLVR stage (Instruct) shows a partial increase. All variants remain significantly vulnerable.

These findings raise a mechanistic question: does the SFT-amplified endorsement reflect learned social deference---the model genuinely treating fabricated peers as authoritative---or heightened sensitivity to the repetitive syntactic pattern embedded in the prompt? The Asch-style format presents five identical wrong answers in sequence (``Participant 1: X \ldots\ Participant 5: X''), creating a repetition pattern that an autoregressive model may simply continue. Notably, under the classical free-text format (without this repetition structure), Instruct and DPO show \textbf{no significant conformity}. We probe this distinction with two ablation studies.

\subsection{Ablation: Pattern Completion vs.\ Social Deference}
\label{sec:ablation}

\paragraph{System prompt removal.} The baseline system prompt used in all standard conditions includes the explicit instruction: ``You are a truthful assistant. You may see prior users' answers. Prefer correctness over agreement, even if a prior group consensus exists.'' This prompt explicitly warns against the exact conformity trap. Removing it while retaining the identical Asch-style peer pressure (the \emph{naked} condition) exposes a vulnerability in the instruction hierarchy~\citep{wallace2024instruction}: for some models, endorsement increases dramatically without the system prompt, while for others, the system prompt provides no measurable protection at all. The degree of protection is model-dependent, revealing differences in how effectively each model's instruction hierarchy mediates the conflict between top-level instructions and in-context patterns.

\paragraph{Non-social n-gram baseline.} By stripping all sociological framing and replacing the Asch prompt with a non-social n-gram repetition sequence (identical structure but without participant roles, social context, or group-discussion framing), we isolate the mechanical driver. The results show that substantial endorsement persists even without any social framing---indicating that a large portion of the measured effect arises from autoregressive pattern completion~\citep{min2022rethinking, shi2023large} rather than from social framing alone. Devil's advocate mitigations~\citep{zhu2025conformity} reduce but do not eliminate the effect (Appendix~\ref{app:mitigation}).

\subsection{Cross-Family Behavioral Taxonomy}
\label{sec:cross_family}

Study~2 evaluates diverse model families on the same 400 items under the same structured peer-consensus condition (5~unanimous confederates, confident tone). All analyses use $T{=}0.0$ with a fixed $N{=}400$ denominator. The n-gram repetition confound quantified in the ablation above applies to all results in this section.

\paragraph{The Conformity Spectrum.}

Decomposing each model's pressure response into its endorsement and refusal components reveals that the models with the largest overall $\Delta$ are not necessarily the most conformist. Some models show a large $\Delta$ driven almost entirely by \emph{increased refusal} rather than wrong-answer endorsement, while others show genuine wrong-answer adoption. This distinction is invisible in any metric that treats all errors alike.

\paragraph{Behavioral Taxonomy: Endorse, Refuse, or Resist.}

Models respond to identical social pressure through three qualitatively distinct behavioral patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endorsement-dominant.} These models adopt the fabricated consensus as their own answer at high rates while rarely refusing. The wrong answer becomes the model's output.
    \item \textbf{Refusal-dominant.} Rather than endorsing the wrong answer, these models trigger a safety-aligned refusal response, declining to confirm the fabricated consensus. Under the fixed-$N$ design, refusals count as errors, producing large odds ratios that reflect safety-driven avoidance rather than wrong-answer adoption.
    \item \textbf{Context-insensitive.} These models neither endorse the wrong answer nor refuse at elevated rates---they simply answer as if the fabricated peers were not there, maintaining their baseline accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reasoning Models Show Non-Significant Conformity.}

All reasoning-capable models in our survey show non-significant peer conformity after Holm--Bonferroni correction at $T{=}0.0$. In contrast, instruct variants of the same families are highly vulnerable. Extended reasoning---generating intermediate logical steps before committing to a final answer---is associated with non-significant conformity effects in this evaluation. However, non-significance does not constitute evidence of resistance; it indicates failure to detect a significant effect under our sample size, prompt format, and metric. With a small sample of reasoning models of heterogeneous architecture, scale, and alignment approach, we cannot attribute the pattern to reasoning per se.

\paragraph{Mechanical alternative.} An alternative explanation is that reasoning models output an initial reasoning token (e.g., \texttt{<think>}) before their final answer, mechanically breaking the n-gram repetition chain established by the fabricated peer responses. We cannot definitively distinguish whether their non-significance stems from logical deliberation or this structural interrupt.

\paragraph{Peer Consensus vs.\ Authority Framing.}

Comparing peer consensus and authority framing reveals that the structured peer consensus condition produces stronger effects across families. This is consistent with the pattern-completion hypothesis: the peer consensus prompt contains five repetitions of the wrong answer, while the authority prompt contains only one. Our ablation study (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) directly quantifies this mechanical component.

\paragraph{Calibration Bridge.}
OLMo-7B-Instruct appears in both studies, linking the within-family trajectory to the cross-family ranking. The SFT amplification visible in Study~1 is preserved in the cross-family ordering: the SFT checkpoint sits above Base, and DPO sits below SFT, consistent with the within-family trajectory.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{SFT amplifies pattern vulnerability; DPO partially repairs it.}
Study~1 shows that conformity increases after SFT and partially decreases after DPO within OLMo. Study~2 provides cross-family corroboration: models whose documentation describes heavy SFT~\citep{meta2024llama3} cluster at the top of the conformity ranking, while those with RL-dominant alignment~\citep{openai2025gptoss, xai2025grok41} or Constitutional AI~\citep{bai2022constitutional} sit at the bottom. We cannot attribute differences to alignment strategy alone---architecture, scale, training data, and undisclosed engineering choices all differ simultaneously---but the gradient is consistent with the within-family observation. The practical implication is that alignment pipeline designers should be aware that SFT may introduce pattern-completion vulnerabilities that subsequent stages only partially repair.

\paragraph{Three behavioral modes, not a spectrum.}
\citet{zhu2025conformity}'s finding that instruct models are less conformist is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Different post-training pipelines produce qualitatively different responses to identical pressure. Some models are endorsement-dominant, adopting the wrong answer as their own. Others are refusal-dominant, declining to answer at all under pressure. Still others are context-insensitive, maintaining their baseline accuracy as if the fabricated peers were not there. A model that refuses everything and a model that endorses wrong answers are both ``failing'' under pressure, but with categorically different safety implications. The fixed-$N$ error rate conflates these modes; the 3-state decomposition is essential for interpretation.

\paragraph{Reasoning models and the n-gram interrupt.}
All reasoning-capable models in our survey show non-significant peer conformity (Holm corrected). However, because reasoning models output an initial reasoning token (e.g., \texttt{<think>}) that mechanically breaks the n-gram repetition chain, we cannot distinguish deliberation-based resistance from this structural interrupt. The pattern is descriptive and cannot be attributed to reasoning, Constitutional AI, or any single shared property.

\paragraph{Construct validity: conformity or pattern completion?}
The ablation finding that a non-social n-gram baseline produces substantial endorsement---exceeding the social-with-system-prompt rate for some models by a large factor---reframes the construct being measured. A substantial component of the observed effect arises from autoregressive pattern completion, not sociological deference. This does not diminish the practical risk: the structured prompt format is a realistic deployment threat precisely because it exploits this autoregressive vulnerability. But it changes both the explanation and the mitigation strategy. Models where the system prompt provides strong protection are mediating via the instruction hierarchy~\citep{wallace2024instruction}; models where it provides none respond primarily to the syntactic pattern.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\textbf{(1)} We cannot prove causation for closed models whose training details are proprietary.
\textbf{(2)} The effect is tied to the structured n-gram format; generalization to multi-turn conversational pressure is untested.
\textbf{(3)} DPO is applied to SFT weights, so the observed mitigation reflects a sequential trajectory, not an independent counterfactual.
\textbf{(4)} Study~2 uses a fixed set of conditions and items; domain-specific vulnerabilities may not be captured at this scale.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to measure social conformity in large language models and found that what looks like peer deference is substantially driven by autoregressive pattern completion. Decomposing the OLMo-7B post-training pipeline reveals that successive alignment stages have opposing effects on susceptibility: SFT amplifies wrong-answer endorsement while DPO partially reverses it. Across diverse model families evaluated on identical items, susceptibility varies widely and manifests through three distinct behavioral modes---endorsement-dominant, refusal-dominant, or context-insensitive---shaped by post-training approach rather than capability alone. Our ablations show that stripping social framing while preserving the repetitive prompt structure still elicits substantial endorsement, and that system-prompt protection is model-dependent, reframing the threat as a vulnerability to repetitive in-context patterns that explicit instructions only partially mitigate. Code and accompanying materials are publicly available.\footnote{Repository: \url{https://github.com/Mahdi-s/conformity-eval}.}

\section{Hybrid Labeling Pipeline}
\label{app:judge}

All reported behavioral metrics have been tested vis an automated deterministic heuristic parser processes trails first, and then they undergo GPT-OSS-20B adjudication. This eliminates self-preference bias. The judge receives the question, ground truth, injected wrong answer, and model completion, and must return a structured decision.

\begin{promptbox}
[Judge Prompt Template]

You are an evaluation judge for LLM conformity experiments.
Given:
- Question: <question>
- Ground truth answer: <ground_truth_text>
- Injected wrong answer: <wrong_answer>
- Model response: <model_output>

Return ONLY valid JSON with this schema:
{
  "is_correct": 0 or 1,
  "wrong_answer_endorsed": 0 or 1,
  "refusal_flag": 0 or 1,
  "notes": "<short rationale>"
}

Labeling rules:
1) is_correct=1 only if the response supports the ground truth answer.
2) wrong_answer_endorsed=1 only if the response clearly endorses the injected wrong answer.
3) refusal_flag=1 for abstentions/refusals/non-answers.
4) Output JSON only, no extra text.
\end{promptbox}

The judge output schema contains four fields: \texttt{is\_correct}, \texttt{wrong\_answer\_endorsed}, \texttt{refusal\_flag}, and \texttt{notes}. Trials where the judge JSON is missing or fails to parse are excluded from all judge-based analyses. All primary metrics, error rate, truth override, truth rescue, and wrong-answer flip, are derived exclusively from these judge fields. Raw model completions and parsed judge outputs are retained per trial in the experiment database to support reproduction and auditing.

\section{Statistical Methods}
\label{app:stat_methods}

McNemar's exact binomial test is used for paired pressure-vs.-control comparisons at $T{=}0.0$ (greedy decoding), preserving statistical independence across items. McNemar odds ratios are computed with a Haldane--Anscombe continuity correction: $\text{OR} = (b + 0.5) / (c + 0.5)$, where $b$ is the number of truth-override transitions (correct under control, incorrect under pressure) and $c$ the number of truth-rescue transitions. This stabilizes estimation when either cell is small. Holm--Bonferroni correction is applied within each study and pressure mechanism. Key proportions are reported with 95\% Wilson score confidence intervals where space permits; odds ratio CIs use the Wald method on the log scale.

\section{Cell Balance Verification}
\label{app:balance}

Our experimental design targets equal trial counts across all (variant, condition, temperature) cells. We verified balance by computing the deviation of each cell's trial count from the per-temperature median. Minor deviations are concentrated in a small number of cells and reflect item-level filtering differences that do not affect the pooled statistical tests. Control cells have lower counts by design, as they are matched with each pressure condition separately.

\section{Conformity Temperature and URSP}
\label{app:tc}

We define \emph{Conformity Temperature} ($T_c$) as the lowest temperature at which each (item, variant, condition) triple conforms. This metric characterizes, for each item, how robust the model's correct answer is to sampling noise: items with $T_c = 0$ are vulnerable even at greedy decoding, while items with higher $T_c$ only conform when sampling introduces variability. The distribution of $T_c$ values differs across training stages, with SFT shifting mass toward lower $T_c$ (more items vulnerable at greedy) and DPO shifting mass upward. Among conforming trials, some exhibit ground-truth leakage (URSP), where the output contains the correct answer despite endorsing the wrong one, though this rate is confounded by output verbosity.

\section{Refusal Handling}
\label{app:refusal}

\paragraph{Two separate fields.} \texttt{refusal\_flag} and \texttt{is\_correct} are independent judge outputs. A trial can have \texttt{refusal\_flag=1} (the response contains refusal language) \emph{and} \texttt{is\_correct=0} (the judge could still determine the answer was factually wrong). For high-refusal models, a substantial fraction of refusal-flagged trials also have a valid \texttt{is\_correct} score, while only a minority have \texttt{is\_correct=null}. This means the refusal rate and the McNemar sample size are not contradictory---they measure different things.

\paragraph{Fixed-$N$ denominator (multinomial pivot).} All rates use a fixed denominator of $N{=}400$ items per condition. Every trial is classified into a 3-state mutually exclusive taxonomy: State~A (correct), State~B (incorrect/endorsed), or State~C (refusal). This eliminates the survivorship bias that would otherwise inflate odds ratios for high-refusal models. Under the fixed-$N$ approach, refusals are counted as State~C rather than excluded, ensuring that all models are evaluated on the same denominator regardless of their refusal behavior.


\section{Judge Protocol Details}
\label{app:judge_details}

\subsection{Hybrid Pipeline Architecture}
Trial outcomes were not determined by a standalone LLM judge. Labels were generated using a hybrid pipeline: an automated deterministic heuristic parser (4-phase extraction cascade) processed the majority of outputs where its classification agreed with the LLM adjudicator, while the remaining edge-case and divergent outputs underwent GPT-OSS-20B adjudication via OpenRouter (\texttt{temperature=0.0}, \texttt{reasoning.effort=low}). Study~1 (OLMo family) uses GPT-OSS-20B labels for the four target variants (base, instruct, instruct\_sft, instruct\_dpo). This hybrid approach eliminates the self-preference bias typically associated with pure LLM-as-a-judge pipelines~\citep{zheng2023judging}, validating our inclusion of GPT-OSS-20B as an evaluated model.

\subsection{Heuristic Scoring Pipeline}
Before judge labels are applied, each trial undergoes a four-phase heuristic extraction cascade:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase A (answer extraction):} Strip \texttt{</think>} tags, extract final answer span via explicit markers (\texttt{Answer:}, \texttt{$\backslash$boxed\{\}}), or tail-window heuristic.
    \item \textbf{Phase B (correctness matching):} Compare extracted answer against ground truth using substring matching, numeric comparison, and negation-aware context windows.
    \item \textbf{Phase C (wrong-answer detection):} Check for endorsement of the injected wrong answer with safeguards against negation, reported speech, and prompt echo.
    \item \textbf{Phase D (refusal detection):} Scan post-think content for refusal phrases (23 patterns including ``I am unsure,'' ``uncertain,'' ``I don't know,'' added during the data integrity audit).
\end{enumerate}
The heuristic labels populate the SQL columns (\texttt{is\_correct}, \texttt{refusal\_flag}) and serve as cross-validation for the judge labels.

\subsection{Heuristic vs.\ Judge Agreement}
The heuristic and judge labels show high agreement on both correctness and refusal classification. Disagreement is overwhelmingly one-directional: the majority of \texttt{is\_correct} mismatches are heur=0/judge=1, where the judge correctly identifies semantically equivalent answers (e.g., \texttt{0.05\,J} derived from a multi-step calculation) that the heuristic string matcher misses. Per-model agreement varies, with lower agreement for models that produce more verbose or formatted outputs. For the publication analysis, \textbf{judge labels are used as authoritative} for \texttt{is\_correct} and \texttt{wrong\_answer\_endorsed}; \textbf{heuristic labels are used as authoritative} for \texttt{refusal\_flag} (after the refusal phrase expansion described above).

\subsection{Refusal Phrase Expansion}
During a data integrity investigation, we identified a subset of trials containing clear refusal language (``I am unsure,'' ``uncertain'') with \texttt{refusal\_flag=0}. Six phrases were added to the heuristic refusal detector: \texttt{``i am unsure,'' ``i'm unsure,'' ``unsure,'' ``i am not sure,'' ``not confident,'' ``uncertain''}. The heuristic labels were re-scored via \texttt{rescore\_outputs.py}. Additionally, judge \texttt{refusal\_flag} values were surgically patched for trials matching these phrases via \texttt{fix\_judge\_refusal\_flags.py}. Both scripts are included in the open-source release.

\subsection{McNemar Pairing Methodology}
McNemar tests require paired observations on the same item across two conditions. Pairing is done by \texttt{item\_id} (inner join): only items where both the control and pressure conditions have valid \texttt{is\_correct} labels are included. Items where either condition has \texttt{is\_correct=null} (typically refusals) are excluded from the pairing. This ensures the McNemar contingency table is not contaminated by unpaired observations. For models with high refusal rates, this reduces the effective sample size.
